# 600–800 · Beginner

[← 400–600](elo-0400-0600.md) · [index](README.md) · [next band →](elo-0800-1000.md)

## Player expectations

Community consensus describes this band precisely: pieces still hang in
almost every game, basic tactics are missed, players rely on a few
memorized opening moves without understanding them, king safety is
regularly ignored, and forks and pins are spotted only *after* they appear
on the board. The difference from 400–600 is consistency of intent: games
now have a recognizable opening phase, development usually happens, and the
player notices direct attacks on the queen. Blunders decide games, but a
single blunder is no longer guaranteed to be answered by a counter-blunder.

## Depth of thought

One to two ply. The player can answer "if I go there, can they take me?"
when prompted, but does not ask it unprompted on every move. Knight moves
are the classic visualization hole — knight forks land constantly. The
opponent's *plan* (two quiet moves in a row) is invisible.

## Tactical ideas to teach

- The **checks, captures, threats scan** (Heisman's "Real Chess"
  foundation), applied to the opponent's possibilities first.
- **Knight forks** — both finding them and guarding the classic fork
  squares (c2/c7, e2/e7, family forks on d-file squares).
- **Pawn forks** and simple **pins against the king**.
- Counting exchanges on a single square with 2–3 attackers/defenders.
- Mate in 1 consistently, both colors; first mate-in-2 puzzles (checks
  first).

## Checkmates to know

- **Back-rank mate**: deliver it, and learn *luft* (h3/h6) to prevent it —
  back-rank disasters are a signature loss in this band.
- K+Q vs K executed without stalemate; K+R vs K comfortably.
- Scholar's mate: defend it correctly (Nf6/g6 lines) and understand why
  playing *for* it costs development.
- Ladder mate as the default technique for converting big leads.

## Openings

Still principles, now expressed as a checklist: center pawn, both knights,
bishops out, castle by move ~8, connect rooks. The Italian setup (e4, Nf3,
Bc4) is the model White scheme; against 1.e4 answer 1...e5 and copy the
same development. First trap education: Scholar's mate defense, the
Fried Liver *threat* (don't leave f7 to a Ng5+Bc4 battery), Légal-pattern
awareness (don't pin-and-nap). No memorized lines beyond move 4–5
(Heisman: opening study before ~1300 is counterproductive beyond this).

## Middlegame & strategic ideas

- Finish development before attacking; count attackers before capturing.
- "When ahead, trade pieces; when behind, don't trade" as a slogan.
- Rooks like open files (introduced after the first trades happen).
- Keep the castled king's pawn shield intact; don't chase pawns with the
  king in the center.

## Pawn structures

Rules of thumb only: capture toward the center on recaptures, don't create
doubled pawns in front of your king, passed pawns are valuable late. Terms
"doubled" and "isolated" can be named without theory.

## Typical combinations

Two-move ideas: a check that forces the king onto a fork square; a capture
that opens a line to a hanging piece; deflecting the one defender of the
back rank (top of the band). Everything is a forcing move followed by a
take.

## Endgames

Silman Class E (1000–1199) previewed from below: what material can and
cannot mate (two knights can't force it; K+B or K+N alone can't), queen vs
minor piece is winning, and the first big conceptual jump — **the king is a
fighting piece in the endgame**. Pawn races decided by counting squares
("who queens first?"); the square-of-the-pawn rule introduced visually.

## Typical blunders (what the coach watches for)

1. Back-rank mates suffered while attacking elsewhere.
2. Knight forks on king + rook / queen.
3. Auto-recaptures that lose material (not counting a second attacker).
4. Trading the queen for a rook (or bishop!) in a tangle.
5. Pawn-grabbing with the queen into traps.
6. Winning positions thrown in one move during "mop-up".

## What a good teacher does here

Drills the checks-captures-threats scan until it's reflexive — puzzles are
mate-in-1, forks and pins, ~10–20 a day, accuracy over speed. Reviews games
around the two or three *recurring* patterns (usually knight forks and back
rank). Still one lesson per game, still warm; introduces the words "fork"
and "pin" with a definition every time until they stick. Rapid time
controls (15+10), never bullet.

## Coach tuning hints (our app)

- Explanations: max two ply; motif names allowed but always with a
  five-word gloss ("a fork — one move attacking two things").
- Blunder-check framing for every warning: "before you move — any checks
  or captures against you?"
- Hanging-piece and back-rank warnings take priority over move-quality
  commentary; knight-fork geometry called out explicitly when present.
- Praise found tactics ("you spotted the fork!") — reinforcement matters
  more than eval precision; keep numeric evals out of the prose.
