## Player expectations

Community consensus describes this band precisely: pieces still hang in
almost every game, basic tactics are missed, players rely on a few
memorized opening moves without understanding them, king safety is
regularly ignored, and forks and pins are spotted only *after* they appear
on the board. The difference from 400–600 is consistency of intent: games
now have a recognizable opening phase, development usually happens, the
player notices direct attacks on the queen, and free pieces get taken when
offered. Blunders still decide games, but a single blunder is no longer
guaranteed to be answered by a counter-blunder — which means the *first*
player to stop hanging pieces in a given game usually wins it.

What a typical game looks like: a reasonable opening for 6–8 moves (some
memorized, some principled), then the first contact — an exchange gets
miscounted or a knight lands a family fork, one side goes a piece up, and
the rest is a mop-up punctuated by one or two scares (a back-rank check
that nearly mates, a stalemate near-miss). Scholar's-mate attempts are
still common *from* players in this band; by its top they stop working and
get retired.

## Rating context

600–800 chess.com rapid corresponds loosely to a new-ish OTB scholastic
player (roughly 300–600 USCF) and to about 1000–1250 on Lichess (the
cross-site gap is widest below 1000 and narrows with rating). "Beginner"
in book titles usually means exactly this band plus the one below.

## Depth of thought

One to two ply. The player can answer "if I go there, can they take me?"
when prompted, but does not ask it unprompted on every move — the habit
exists, the *reflex* doesn't. Knight moves are the classic visualization
hole: knight forks land constantly, because a knight two moves away is
functionally invisible. The opponent's *plan* (two quiet moves in a row
building toward something) is entirely off-screen, and that's fine — at
this depth the curriculum is forcing moves only.

### The thought process to teach

Heisman's "Real Chess" foundation, cut down to the band's size — after the
opponent moves, then again before committing your own move:

> **Checks, captures, threats — theirs first, then mine.**

Three words, in that order, every move. The band's characteristic failure
is applying it on the exciting moves and skipping it on "obvious" ones
(recaptures, developing moves) — which is exactly where the forks land.
Teachers make the student *say the opponent's best reply out loud* before
touching the piece; our coach can mimic this with a "what does this allow?"
prompt on candidate moves.

## Tactical ideas to teach

- The **checks, captures, threats scan** (above), applied to the
  opponent's possibilities first.
- **Knight forks** — both finding them and guarding the classic fork
  squares: c2/c7 and e2/e7 (fork king + rook when the queen's gone or the
  king's in the center), f2/f7, and the "family fork" squares in front of
  a castled king (e.g. a knight reaching e7+/e2+ or f6+/f3+ geometry).
  Drill both directions: 15–20 fork puzzles, then a game where the only
  extra task is "check every knight hop each move".
- **Pawn forks** (the two-square pawn advance hitting two pieces) and
  simple **pins against the king** (absolute pins; the pinned piece is a
  fake defender — this idea alone wins material repeatedly at 700).
- **Counting** exchanges on a single square with 2–3 attackers/defenders:
  captures happen cheapest-piece-first; stop when taking loses value.
- **Mate in 1 consistently, both colors**; first mate-in-2 puzzles with
  the instruction "check every check first".
- **Skewer** introduced as "a pin backwards" (king in front, loot behind).

## Checkmates to know

- **Back-rank mate**: deliver it, and learn *luft* (h3/h6 at a calm
  moment) to prevent it — back-rank disasters are a signature loss of this
  band. The teaching form: "your rook's job includes staying home until
  the king has a hole."
- **K+Q vs K executed without stalemate** and K+R vs K comfortably (box
  method: rook fences, king walks, rook checks only when kings face).
- **Scholar's mate handled both ways**: defend it correctly (2...Nc6
  3...g6 vs Qh5; ...Nf6 lines vs Qf3), and understand why playing *for* it
  costs development — the retirement of the Scholar's attempt is a rite of
  passage out of this band.
- **Ladder mate** as the default technique for converting big leads.
- **Smothered mate — recognize the shape** (knight check, king boxed by
  its own pieces). Executing the full Philidor's Legacy is the next
  band's material; here it's "corner + own pawns = knight danger".
- First **Q+B battery** ideas against h7/h2 (queen and bishop lined up on
  the b1–h7 diagonal): count defenders of h7 before "attacking".

## Openings

Still principles, now expressed as a repeatable checklist: center pawn,
both knights, both bishops out, castle by move ~8, connect rooks, *then*
look for a plan. The model White scheme is the **Italian setup**: 1.e4 e5
2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 — every move develops, aims at the center, and teaches the
f7 story. As Black: answer 1.e4 with 1...e5 and copy the same development;
answer 1.d4 with 1...d5 and develop symmetrically (Bf5 or e6 + Be7, then
castle).

First trap education (defense before offense):

- **Scholar's mate** defense (above) — non-negotiable.
- **Fried Liver awareness**: after 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Nf6 4.Ng5, know
  that 4...d5 5.exd5 *Nxd5?* invites 6.Nxf7! — the safe rule at this level
  is "don't allow Ng5 hitting f7 unattended" (3...Bc5 first, or know
  5...Na5 exists when older).
- **Légal's pattern awareness** ("don't pin and nap"): a bishop pinning
  Nf3 to the queen does not make Nxe5 impossible — count what happens
  after the "impossible" knight move (Nxe5! Bxd1?? Bxf7+ Ke7 Nd5#). Both
  a trap to avoid falling into and a first taste of calculation.

No memorized lines beyond move 4–5. Heisman's guidance applies from here
until ~1300–1400: opening-line study beyond traps + principles is
counterproductive — every minute of it is a minute not spent on the
tactics that actually decide this band's games.

## Middlegame & strategic ideas

- **Finish development before attacking**; count attackers vs defenders
  before capturing anything defended.
- "**When ahead, trade pieces; when behind, don't trade**" as a slogan —
  with the addendum "trade pieces, not pawns, when ahead".
- **Rooks like open files** (introduced after the first trades happen, not
  as an abstraction).
- Keep the castled king's pawn shield intact; don't chase pawns with the
  king in the center; don't open the position while uncastled.
- First target talk: f7/f2 while the king's in the center; h7/h2 after
  castling — and the mirror ("my f2 is their target").

## Pawn structures

Rules of thumb only, each tied to a safety story:

- Capture **toward the center** on recaptures (default, not dogma).
- Don't create doubled pawns **in front of your own king**; elsewhere
  they're often fine — nuance arrives two bands later.
- **Passed pawns are valuable late**: in endings, the extra outside pawn
  is often the whole game.
- The terms "doubled" and "isolated" can be named without theory, purely
  descriptively.

## Typical combinations

Two-move ideas — one forcing move, then a take:

1. **Check that forces the king onto a fork square**, then the knight
   family-fork scoops the rook (the band's signature win *and* loss).
2. **Capture that opens a line** to a hanging piece behind.
3. **Deflect the back-rank defender** (top of the band): trade off or
   chase the one rook guarding the mate, then Rd8#-style finishes.
4. **Pawn fork after a forced recapture**: exchange on a square, the
   recapturing piece stands next to a pawn's double-step.

Everything is "forcing move + take". Three-move combinations with a quiet
move in them belong two bands up.

## Endgames

Silman's Beginner class consolidated, Class E (1000–1199) previewed:

- **What can and cannot mate** a lone king: K+Q, K+R, K+2B yes; K+B or
  K+N alone never; K+2N cannot *force* it. Practical consequence taught
  as a rule: "if trading into K+N vs K, it's a draw — count first."
- **Queen vs minor piece** is winning: centralize, fork loose bits, box.
- **The king is a fighting piece in the endgame** — the first big
  conceptual jump. Once queens are off, the king walks to the center.
- **Pawn races** decided by counting ("who queens first?"), with the
  **square of the pawn** introduced visually (draw the diagonal from the
  pawn to the promotion rank; if the defending king can step inside the
  square, it catches the pawn).
- Promote-and-mate remains the default winning plan: extra material →
  trade pieces → push a passer → new queen → ladder/box mate.

## Time management & practical play

- 15+10 rapid as the standard; the increment specifically trains the
  "sit on your hands" habit without flag panic.
- Pacing rule for the band: forcing positions (a capture or check on the
  board) deserve a real think; quiet developing moves shouldn't burn
  minutes. The classic failure is inverted spending — 3 minutes on move 5,
  3 seconds on the recapture that loses a rook.
- After every opponent capture: **pause**. Auto-recapture is this band's
  most expensive reflex (see zwischenzug, next band).

## Typical blunders (what the coach watches for)

1. Back-rank mates suffered while attacking elsewhere.
2. Knight forks on king + rook / king + queen (both missed and allowed).
3. Auto-recaptures that lose material (a second attacker was aimed at the
   square).
4. Trading the queen for a rook (or a bishop!) in a tangle of pieces.
5. Pawn-grabbing with the queen into loss of the queen or fatal
   development deficit.
6. Winning positions thrown in one move during "mop-up" (carelessness
   once ahead — the count says the game is over, the board disagrees).
7. Pins ignored: "defended" by a pinned piece, or moving a pinned piece.

## A study week at 600–800

- 4–5 rapid games (15+10) with the checks/captures/threats scan enforced;
  one game reviewed to find the two or three *recurring* patterns (usually
  knight forks and back rank — track them by name).
- 10–20 tactics puzzles per day, restricted themes: forks, pins,
  mate-in-1, early mate-in-2; accuracy over speed, and every miss replayed
  from the diagram until the pattern is seen, not remembered.
- One endgame rep per week: K+R vs K until boring, then Q vs minor piece.
- 10 minutes of trap defense: Scholar's, Fried Liver threat, Légal —
  set them up on a board, play both sides.
- Explicitly zero opening study beyond the checklist and the traps.

## What a good teacher does here

Drills the checks-captures-threats scan until it's reflexive; reviews games
around the two or three *recurring* patterns rather than every error; still
one lesson per game, still warm. Introduces the words "fork" and "pin" with
a definition every time until they stick ("a fork — one move attacking two
things"). Insists on rapid time controls and full-attention games; praises
*noticing* ("you saw his threat and stopped it") at least as often as
winning material, because noticing is the skill under construction.

## Graduation checklist (ready for 800–1000 when…)

- [ ] The opponent's checks/captures/threats are scanned on most moves
      without prompting (audit: can narrate it during a review).
- [ ] Knight-fork losses down to rare accidents; fork puzzles ~90%.
- [ ] Back rank: luft made proactively in most games; no back-rank losses
      in the last ~15 games.
- [ ] Development checklist completes by move 10 in most games (all
      minors out, castled).
- [ ] Scholar's mate attempts fully retired; its defense automatic.
- [ ] Simple counting (2 attackers vs 1 defender) errors are rare.

## Coach tuning hints (our app)

- Explanations: max two ply; motif names allowed but always with a
  five-word gloss ("a fork — one move attacking two things").
- Blunder-check framing for every warning: "before you move — any checks
  or captures against you?"
- Hanging-piece and back-rank warnings take priority over move-quality
  commentary; knight-fork geometry called out explicitly whenever a fork
  is one hop away (either color).
- Praise found tactics ("you spotted the fork!") — reinforcement matters
  more than eval precision; keep numeric evals out of the prose entirely.
- On auto-recapture moments (opponent just captured), slow the player
  down: "before recapturing — any checks for you? any second attacker
  aimed here?"
