## Player expectations

Rating literature describes 1800–2000 as "club-level strength — capable
of accurate play in all phases", players who "know the game inside out,
give quite objective evaluations of positions, and take advantage of even
the tiniest mistakes". The band sits directly below Expert/Candidate
Master (2000–2200). Games between such players are decided by small
accumulating slips, conversion technique, preparation depth, and
psychology (clock handling, resilience after errors) — the improvement
literature for this range is explicitly about "consistency, precision,
stronger calculation, plus psychological resilience".

GM Pert's dedicated 1800–2000 mistakes bracket names the residual leaks:
**when to exchange, converting an advantage, passive pieces, anticipating
the opponent's plans, and opening/pawn-structure understanding**. The
arithmetic of the climb is sobering and worth teaching: to move from 1800
to 2000 you must turn a 50% score against 1800s into ~75% — two losses
per eight games converted into wins, with no new tactics to harvest.
One-move tactical accidents are rare enough that a single one usually
decides the game.

What a typical game looks like: real opening preparation meeting real
opening preparation; a middlegame where both sides know the structure's
plans and the fight is about *move-order finesse and prophylaxis*; an
advantage measured in fractions of a pawn nursed (or squandered) across
20 moves; and a technical phase where theoretical endgame knowledge and
"do not hurry" either close the deal or let the defender's resources
(fortress, perpetual, Vancura) save it.

## Rating context

1800–2000 chess.com rapid ≈ roughly 1700–1900 USCF, ≈ 2100–2250 Lichess
rapid, brushing FIDE ~1800–2000 (the scales converge near the top). OTB
this is a top club board / Class A–Expert; the last purely amateur band.

## Depth of thought

Seven to ten ply in forcing lines and more when the position demands it
(Steps 6 exercises run 9–13 ply). Recall the research framing: masters
average ~9–11 ply of planning in analysis tasks and grandmasters
calculate only slightly deeper than candidate masters but far more
accurately — beyond this band, gains come from **accuracy, selection and
evaluation**, not raw depth. Training targets accordingly: stepping-stone
positions (re-anchoring the mind's-eye board mid-line), comparison of
resulting positions rather than re-calculation, and knowing when NOT to
calculate (technique positions, where schematic thinking is faster and
safer).

### The thought process to teach

Nothing new is added to the loop; the work is making it *cheap*:

1. **Mode selection first**: is this a calculation position, a technique
   position, or a decision position (two playable plans)? Each mode has
   a different time price.
2. Calculation positions: candidates, forcingness, verification —
   with pruning by evaluation ("this branch ends worse than my quiet
   alternative; abandon it half-verified").
3. Technique positions: schematic thinking — name the target
   configuration ("rook behind the passer, king on e5, pawn on the 6th")
   and walk there, checking only the opponent's active tries.
4. Decision positions: pick by statistics (which structure do I score
   in?), by opponent (what does *he* hate?), and by clock — and commit
   without residue.

## Tactical ideas to teach

- **Deep forcing lines containing quiet moves and zugzwang motifs** —
  study-like material; the Steps 6 exercise diet.
- **Defensive resources at depth**: only-move sequences, desperado
  cascades, fortress construction, stalemate tricks as a saving weapon
  (rook desperados in "lost" endings).
- **Speculative vs sound sacrifice judgment**: compensation categories
  (structure, king exposure, piece paralysis, development lead) and
  their shelf lives; the intuition audit ("would I need this to be
  sound, or is it sound?").
- **Calculation economy**: pruning by evaluation, candidate ordering by
  refutation-likelihood, clock-aware depth budgets.
- Counter-tactics as prophylaxis: seeing the opponent's combination two
  moves before it exists and removing its preconditions.

## Checkmates & attack

New named mates stopped mattering long ago; the material is **attacking
method** (Vuković-grade):

- Attack only with more force than the defense; the three phases —
  accumulation → line-opening → execution — managed as a resource
  budget (every attacking move that doesn't add force or open lines is
  a tempo lost from the storm).
- **The standard sacrifices known cold, with conditions**: Bxh7+ (Greek
  gift and its Kg6/Kh6 refutation branches), the double-bishop
  sacrifice (Lasker–Bauer: queen+rook must arrive before ...f8-flight),
  Nxg7/Bxg7 and Nf5 vs the fianchetto, Rxf6-style demolitions of the
  f6-knight shield, sacs on e6/f7 in Sicilian structures.
- **The defensive mirror at equal weight**: prophylactic king safety
  (luft chosen by color complex, not habit), when to run the king
  early, defensive piece returns (giving back the exchange to kill the
  attack), and defending by trading the attack's *key* piece.
- Attack as strategy: playing for the attack from the structure (chain
  points at the king → storm is positionally justified) rather than
  from mood — and the discipline to switch targets when the defense
  overcommits.

## Openings

A personal repertoire *file* per line: critical continuations 12+ moves
where theory demands it, each line ending in a middlegame tabiya with
known plans and a model game.

- **Preparation routine**: check games in your lines periodically; patch
  holes found in your own games the same week.
- Understanding beats memory even here, but memory is no longer optional
  in sharp mainlines (the Najdorf/KID/Grünfeld class of projects).
- **Second systems vs everything important**, chosen to diversify
  structures (one open and one closed answer to 1.e4) — both as
  surprise weapons and as insurance against preparation.
- Opponent-specific preparation becomes real where games are announced
  (club matches, tournaments): one evening per opponent, their lines,
  one prepared idea.
- The Pert-bracket warning applies: opening *understanding* (structures,
  plans) still outranks line-memorizing even at 1800–2000 — the leak is
  "openings and pawn structure", not "insufficient theory".

## Middlegame & strategic ideas

- **Deep prophylaxis**: playing against the opponent's *plan*, not
  their moves (Karpov/Petrosian model games; the training exercise —
  predict the opponent's next three moves, not your own).
- **Positional pawn sacrifices** for squares, files or blockade-breaking
  (the b4/d5/e6 pawn levers given up for permanent assets).
- **Initiative husbandry**: converting a temporary initiative into
  something permanent (structure, material, a bind) before it
  evaporates — with the three-move audit ("if my next three moves don't
  cash this, it's gone").
- **Simplification technique as a weapon**: choosing *which* winning
  ending to steer for; avoiding the opponent's drawing setups
  (OCB, fortress-prone rook endings) — Pert's "when to exchange" theme
  at its highest form.
- **Structure transformations mid-game** as deliberate strategy
  (accepting an IQP to activate pieces, then liquidating it; trading
  into hanging pawns to fix targets).
- **Playing for two results** with the safer side of an imbalance;
  pressing without risk as a learned skill (Carlsen-style grinding as
  the model).
- The **principle of two weaknesses** as the default conversion engine.

## Pawn structures

The entire catalog with transformation fluency: Carlsbad ↔ open-file
battles, IQP ↔ hanging pawns ↔ isolated pair conversions, Maroczy and
Hedgehog springs, Benoni/KID dynamic chains, Stonewall plans, doubled-
pawn compensation cases. The key skill is **choosing trades that steer
between structures** ("this exchange converts their IQP game into a won
knight-vs-bad-bishop ending"). Structure knowledge now feeds three
decisions simultaneously: the opening repertoire (which structures do I
score in?), middlegame trades (which transformations favor me?), and
conversion routes (which endings does this structure produce?). This is
the band where systematic structure study (Rios' *Chess Structures*-type
material, aimed at 1800+) pays in full.

## Typical combinations

- **Long combinations with intermediate quiet moves**, calculated to an
  *evaluated* quiescent position, not to material count.
- **Positional exchange sacrifices in the Petrosian style**
  (prophylactic, long-term, no forced payoff) as part of the normal
  toolkit — plus the judgment to decline them when the compensation
  clock runs slow.
- **Breakthrough combinations in "blocked" positions**: pawn-storm
  demolitions, piece sacs to create connected passers, the b5/g5 lever
  sacs in bind structures.
- **Endgame combinations**: transitions into won K+P endings calculated
  with tempo-counting precision; fortress-breaking maneuvers
  (triangulation into zugzwang, sacrificial breaches of the wall).
- Defensive combinations: desperado sequences and stalemate nets that
  save half-points from lost positions.

## Endgames

Silman Class A mastered, Expert material (2000–2199) begun; *100
Endgames You Must Know* essentially complete:

- **Rook endgames at full practical strength**: Lucena/Philidor families
  including all rook-pawn cases, rook in front of its own pawn (the
  checking-distance rules), **Vancura automatic**, cutting off by files
  vs ranks, the short-side/long-side defense assignments, R+P(4th/5th)
  vs R theory (Class A's core chapter).
- **"Strange races"** (Class A): queening races with mutual promotions,
  underpromotion tricks, king-walk paradoxes.
- **Minor-piece endings with precision**: good/bad bishop conversions,
  N vs B by structure, **fortresses in bishop-up endings** (Class A's
  OCB material) — both building them and knowing which are breachable.
- **Queen endings introduced properly** (Expert set): perpetual zones,
  passed-pawn priority over material, the centralized queen's dual
  role.
- **Method over moves**: "do not hurry", the two-weaknesses grind,
  schematic thinking (target positions, not move sequences), Silman's
  Expert "flowchart" habit — taking a simple position into deeper water
  only when the standard result is secured.

## Time management & practical play

- Classical chess where available; the band's skills need the clock
  room. Rapid for volume, blitz for fun only.
- **Clock as strategy**: steering toward complexity when the opponent
  is short; banking time in known theory to spend at the transition
  moments; perfectionism named as the band's characteristic clock
  disease (spending 20 minutes choosing between two winning moves).
- **Psychological routines are now curriculum, not garnish**: the
  post-mistake reset ritual; the "no result-watching" rule mid-game;
  playing the position, not the rating; the second-error statistic
  (most collapses happen within three moves of the first slip) posted
  where the player can see it.
- Tournament craft: energy management across rounds, draw offers as
  strategic decisions, when to press and when to bank the half point.

## Typical blunders (what the coach watches for)

1. Small eval slips accumulating unnoticed (the −0.3 that becomes −1.5
   by move 35).
2. Over-pressing equal positions against lower-rated opponents.
3. Preparation gaps punished in sharp lines.
4. Clock-management failures: perfectionism early, panic late.
5. Psychological cascades: the second error following the first within
   three moves.
6. Conversion sloppiness: allowing counterplay when a "boring" win
   existed (the Pert-bracket flagship).
7. Wrong simplifications: entering the drawing ending instead of the
   winning one (OCB and rook-ending traps).

## A study week at 1800–2000

The 50/30/10/10 baseline, personalized by the player's statistics, with
~45% of total chess time spent *playing*:

- **Calculation (~50% of study)**: endgame studies and composition-
  grade puzzles 3×/week at full effort with written lines; deep
  defensive sets; regular "refute this sacrifice" work.
- **Openings (~30%)**: repertoire files maintained to 12+ moves in
  critical lines with tabiyas and model games; same-week hole-patching;
  periodic line health-checks against recent master games.
- **Middlegame (~10%)**: prophylaxis prediction training on
  Karpov/Petrosian games; annotated study of *own* structures at master
  level; conversion post-mortems on every own won-then-not-won game.
- **Endgame (~10%)**: 100-Endgames completion and speed-testing (known
  positions played against the engine on increment); queen-ending
  introduction; fortress catalog.
- Play 2–4 serious games (classical when possible); every game
  annotated to Dojo standard; the statistics sheet (loss causes by
  phase, structure, clock state) updated and *believed*.

## What a good teacher does here

Acts closer to a second than a schoolteacher: honest analytical
arguments, disputed evaluations settled on the board, preparation help
in the student's lines. Training is statistics-driven (which phase,
which structures, which time situations leak points) and includes
sparring from prepared critical positions, endgame grinds against the
clock, and explicit psychological routines (post-mistake reset ritual,
time-budget checkpoints). Praise is unnecessary; precision and honesty
are the service.

## Graduation checklist (ready for 2000+ / Expert when…)

- [ ] Score vs 1800-rated opposition sustained at ~70%+ over a real
      sample; vs 2000s no longer a losing record.
- [ ] Conversion: positions +2 or better at move 30 won at ≥90%;
      "boring win chosen over interesting mess" narratable from own
      games.
- [ ] Rook-endgame theory (Lucena/Philidor/Vancura/rook-pawn cases)
      executed on increment without thought; queen-ending basics known.
- [ ] Prophylaxis prediction: opponent's plan named correctly most
      moves in blind-tested reviews.
- [ ] Repertoire files complete, current, and tested in serious games;
      no loss-cause tagged "opening surprise" in the last quarter.
- [ ] Clock discipline: no game lost on a sub-2-minute panic in the
      last quarter; perfectionism spending visibly reduced.
- [ ] The psychology sheet holds: no second-error-within-three-moves
      cascade in recent losses.

## Coach tuning hints (our app)

- Explanations: full PVs with evals; unrestricted vocabulary; terse.
- Highest-value behaviors: quantify ("this keeps +1.4; the game move
  drops to +0.4"), name the demanded plan and the opponent's
  counterplay window, flag conversion moments and time-pressure-typical
  errors.
- Challenge the player: "find the refutation of ...Nxe4 before moving
  on" style prompts are appropriate (future quiz mode).
- Skip praise entirely unless a genuinely hard resource was found;
  never simplify vocabulary or hide lines.
- Simplification audit: at every liquidation decision, name the ending
  being entered and its theoretical result — the band's most expensive
  residual error is choosing the drawable win.
- Track and surface the player's own statistics (phase leaks, structure
  scores, clock-state errors) — at this level the coach's edge is
  bookkeeping, not chess.
