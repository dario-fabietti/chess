## Player expectations

The "improving amateur" range. One-move threats are usually seen; the
standard motifs are solved in puzzles; development and castling happen by
habit; the player has a spoken opening plan ("Italian, castle, c3-d4").
What's missing is reliability under game conditions and any real
middlegame direction — plans remain vague ("attack the king", "develop
and see what happens").

Study-plan literature for this band is unanimous: **tactics are ~70% of
the improvement lever** here. Games are still mostly decided by a missed
simple tactic — now two moves deep instead of one — or by the classic
cause-chain: no plan → aimless move → weakening → tactic appears. Time
management remains shaky (fast when winning, frozen when worse).

What a typical game looks like: a book-ish opening for 8–10 moves; a
middlegame of plausible moves where both sides shuffle until one creates a
threat the other misses; material imbalance; then a conversion phase that
works if it's simple ("trade and push") and wobbles if it needs technique.
The first *real draws* appear — opposite-colored bishops, dead-equal rook
endings — and they confuse the band ("I was up a pawn, why is this
drawn?"), which is exactly why Class D endgame material starts now.

## Rating context

1000–1200 chess.com rapid ≈ roughly 900–1150 USCF, ≈ 1400–1600 Lichess
rapid. Crossing 1200 puts a player in roughly the **top 30% of active
chess.com players** — the psychological line where "beginner" genuinely
stops applying, whatever internet culture says.

## Depth of thought

Two to four ply — the core Steps 2–3 range. Forced sequences of captures
and checks can be followed four ply out; a single quiet move inside a line
still breaks visualization (the position "resets" in the mind's eye). The
discipline of checking *all* checks and captures — not the first
attractive one — is the growth edge, together with Steps 3's explicit
skill: **thinking ahead** (hold the changed position, then scan it as if
it were on the board).

### The thought process to teach

Heisman's Real Chess loop, now complete and written down:

1. **What are all the things their last move does?** (threats created,
   defenders abandoned, lines opened/closed — always at least two
   answers.)
2. **Can I meet the threat and improve at the same time?** (defend by
   developing; the best defensive move often attacks.)
3. **Candidates**: my checks, captures, threats — *list before choosing*.
4. **Calculate the chosen candidate**: their best reply (not the reply I
   want), my answer to it.
5. **Blunder-check**: after my move, their checks/captures/threats — can
   I meet each one?

The band's task is not learning this list — it's *executing it every
move for a whole game*, which is a stamina skill. Reviews audit exactly
where in the game the loop stopped running (usually right after gaining
an advantage).

## Tactical ideas to teach

- **Fluency drills on the full Step-2 motif set** until fast and
  reliable: double attack with every piece, pin (absolute vs relative),
  skewer, discovered attack/check, back rank, mate in 2. Fluency means:
  seen in ≤30 seconds in puzzles, spotted both ways in games.
- **Elimination of the defense as a family** (the band's new content):
  - *capture* the defender (even at a temporary material cost),
  - *deflect* it (force it to a square where it stops guarding),
  - *overload* it (give it a second job it can't do),
  - *chase* it (attack it until it must abandon the post).
- **Zwischenzug taught explicitly**, first as defense ("before you
  recapture, check checks"), then as a weapon (insert the stronger
  forcing move before recapturing).
- **Simplifying combinations**: trade down into a won pawn ending *on
  purpose* — the first time tactics and endgame knowledge cooperate.
- Counting mastery: multi-piece exchanges on one square, including the
  "pinned defender doesn't count" correction.

## Checkmates to know

- **Smothered mate executed** from typical middlegame positions (the
  Qg8+!! Philidor's Legacy finish, plus the quieter Nf7/Nd7 smothers
  against a cornered king).
- **Anastasia's, Arabian, corridor and hook mates** as recognized shapes
  that generate *plans* ("his king is on the h-file with a knight ready
  for e7 — Anastasia geometry, look for the rook check").
- **Greek gift awareness — defensive first**: what makes Bxh7+ work
  against you (your knight chased off f6, no bishop able to cover the
  b1–h7 diagonal, your queen too far to defend h5/h7); keep a defender's
  eye on the h7/h2 sacrifice conditions before pushing kingside pawns.
- **Mating nets**: cut off flight squares *before* checking — the
  band's checks are still often "checking for checking's sake", and net
  thinking is the cure.
- Back-rank technique matures: doubling on the file before invading,
  counting *all* defenders of the mate square.

## Openings

A consistent **1 + 1 + 1 repertoire** played every game for months
(repertoire guides for the band: commit for ≥3 months or ~50 games;
specialists beat generalists at this level).

- **White — Italian recommended**: 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5 4.c3 Nf6
  5.d4 (or the quieter 4.d3 + O-O + Re1 plan). Attach the *plan*, not
  just moves: c3+d4 builds the center; d3 systems maneuver (Nbd2–f1–g3)
  and break with d4 later.
- **Vs 1.e4 — stay with 1...e5** (the Italian/Two Knights structures
  from the Black side; know the Fried Liver defense 5...Na5 cold), or
  the **Caro-Kann** (1...c6 2...d5, develop the c8-bishop *before* e6)
  for players who hate early chaos.
- **Vs 1.d4 — QGD setup**: d5, e6, Nf6, Be7, O-O, then ...c5 or ...c6
  with the light-squared bishop's development as the known problem to
  solve (...b6+Bb7 or ...Bd7–e8–g6 patterns can wait).
- Depth: main line 5–8 moves **with the plan attached**. Against gambits:
  accept once, learn why, then choose the safe decline (e.g. vs the
  King's Gambit, 2...Bc5 is a fine "decline and develop" answer at this
  level).
- After each game, look up **only the moment you left known territory** —
  one move, one idea, back to tactics. This is the entire opening study
  budget (~15% of study time).

## Middlegame & strategic ideas

- Plans get names and triggers:
  - open file → rook(s) there, double, invade on the 7th;
  - outpost (a square no enemy pawn can ever hit) → knight;
  - opposite-side castling → pawn storm race (push where they live);
  - same-side castling → piece pressure, no pawn storms.
- **The attackers-vs-defenders audit** before committing to any attack —
  and abandoning attacks the audit rejects.
- Weak color complexes in embryo: "you traded the bishop that guards
  those squares — now g6/f5 belong to him."
- When the position is quiet: **improve the worst piece** (the method:
  name your worst piece, name its dream square, count the moves to get
  there, check what it costs).
- First prophylactic question (informal): "if I do nothing this move,
  what does he play?" — planted here, harvested at 1400+.

## Pawn structures

- **IQP met from the defending side** (it arises constantly from QGD/
  Caro structures): blockade the square in front (knight on d5/d4),
  trade minor pieces, keep the queen for the endgame... then win the pawn
  in the ending. The attacker's kit is next band's material.
- **Pawn chains**: "attack the base" as the first chain concept
  (French-style chains via Caro/French move orders; the base at d4/e3 is
  hit by ...c5/...f6 style levers).
- **Healthy vs crippled majorities**: 3v2 makes a passer, doubled 3v2
  often doesn't — checked before every mass trade.
- When doubled pawns are fine: toward the center, opening a file you
  use, or as capture-toward-center byproducts.
- The **candidate pawn** rule previewed: in a majority, advance the pawn
  with no opponent in front of it first.

## Typical combinations

Three-move combinations built from two motifs — the band's defining
tactical growth:

1. **Remove the defender → fork**: Rxe6 (the fork's only guard), then
   Nd5+ family.
2. **Deflection → back-rank mate**: Qb8+!-style deflections; the queen
   sac that forces the guarding rook off the file.
3. **Pin → pile-up → win of the pinned piece**: add the pawn push last.
4. **Discovered check → grab**: the front piece captures a rook/queen
   while the check paralyzes.
5. **The f7/f2 sacrifice, calculated**: Bxf7+/Nxf7 followed by a fork or
   a mating attack — now with the defender count and the escape squares
   verified *before* the sacrifice, not after.

## Endgames

Silman Class E consolidated, Class D (1200–1399) begun:

- **Opposition used correctly**, not just known: direct opposition,
  taking it vs losing it, and the rule "the attacking king in front of
  its pawn wins (except rook-pawns) *if* it has the opposition… and with
  the pawn on the 5th+ even without it" built through play, not
  memorized.
- **K+P vs K complete** (non-rook-pawn wins, all the draws): defender's
  method (stay in front, take the opposition), attacker's method (king
  ahead of pawn, opposition, don't advance the pawn too early).
- **Key squares** introduced as the cleaner way to think: for a pawn on
  e4, the key squares are d6/e6/f6 — get the king there and the win
  plays itself.
- **Minor piece vs lone pawn**: stopping it (drop anchor in front),
  when the piece must be sacrificed for it, knight-specific stopping
  geometry (knights stop pawns best from in front, worst from the side).
- **"Fox in the chicken coop"** technique names (Silman's Class D set)
  for rook-and-pawn mop-ups.
- Pawn races: count with checks along the way ("does my queen arrive
  with check?") — the refinement that settles the races the square rule
  can't.

## Time management & practical play

- 15+10 minimum; **30+20 recommended** as the main improvement control
  (Belenkaya's 1000–1400 guide and ChessMood's intermediate plan both
  prescribe slower rapid/classical for this exact band).
- Budget by phase: opening ≤20% of clock, critical middlegame moments
  are what the bank is *for*, keep 20%+ for the ending.
- One-result thinking is the band's tell: blitzing while winning,
  freezing while worse. Concrete counter-habit: after any material gain,
  the *next three moves* get full blunder-checks (this is where won
  games are lost).
- Tag every loss by cause — tactic missed / time / opening disaster /
  endgame technique — and let the tags pick next week's training.

## Typical blunders (what the coach watches for)

1. Two-move oversights: the reply-to-the-reply is missed.
2. Recapture automatism vs zwischenzug (both suffering it and missing
   their own).
3. Attacks continued after the defender count says stop.
4. Trading into lost pawn endings (or declining trades into won ones) —
   endgame knowledge not yet consulted *during* the middlegame.
5. One-result clock usage: fast when winning, frozen when worse.
6. Opening leaves book on move 5 and drifts without a plan for 10 moves.
7. The post-advantage lapse: the loop stops running right after winning
   material.

## A study week at 1000–1200

Following the ChessMood 1000–1500 split (70/15/15) around 3–5 slow games:

- **Tactics (~70% of study)**: 20–30 puzzles/day at accuracy pace;
  themed blocks (this week: elimination of the defender; next:
  zwischenzug); every miss re-solved from the diagram after 2 days.
- **Openings (~15%)**: patch exactly the holes your own games exposed;
  one model game in your main line per week, played through fast, twice.
- **Middlegame+endgame (~15%)**: one Class D/E endgame session (K+P vs K
  vs the engine from both sides until 100%); one "improve the worst
  piece" exercise on positions from your own games.
- Play 3–5 games at 15+10/30+20; annotate one per week in sentences,
  including *the plan you had* at three different moments (even if the
  plan was bad — especially then).

## What a good teacher does here

Enforces the written thought process and audits it during reviews ("show
me the blunder-check on this move"). Has the student tag every loss by
cause so training targets facts, not feelings. Daily puzzle blocks at
accuracy pace; weekly annotated game; endgame curriculum runs in parallel
because opponents at this level can't punish endgame study's opportunity
cost — but they absolutely lose to its results. Begins asking "what was
your plan with this move?" and expecting a sentence, not a shrug. Rapid
or slower only.

## Graduation checklist (ready for 1200–1400 when…)

- [ ] Standard-motif puzzles (~1200-rated) solved fast and ~90% accurate;
      elimination-of-the-defender family fluent.
- [ ] Zwischenzug: no auto-recapture losses in the last ~20 games; at
      least one in-between move *played* on purpose.
- [ ] K+P vs K perfect from both sides against an engine; opposition
      explained out loud correctly.
- [ ] Loss tags show tactics-missed dropping below ~50% of losses.
- [ ] Every reviewed game shows a nameable plan in the middlegame
      ("rooks to the c-file", "trade into the won ending"), even if
      imperfect.
- [ ] Repertoire trio stable ≥3 months; out-of-book moments looked up
      after games as a habit.

## Coach tuning hints (our app)

- Explanations: 3–4 ply; full basic-motif vocabulary bare; introduce
  "deflection"/"overload" with a short gloss the first time per session.
- Move reviews can now reference the *cause* class ("this lost material
  to a two-move tactic: his bishop was overloaded").
- One main lesson per review still applies — pick the most instructive
  error, not all of them.
- Start attaching plans to praise ("good — that rook belongs on the open
  c-file") and quoting the opponent's best threat by name.
- Numeric evals may appear in parentheses, still translated ("winning a
  pawn (+1.1)").
- Post-advantage vigilance: after the player wins material, raise the
  warning sensitivity for the next few moves and say why ("most games
  are lost right after they're won — blunder-check these").
