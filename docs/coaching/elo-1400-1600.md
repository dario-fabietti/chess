## Player expectations

The best-documented plateau in amateur chess. Players here have "learned
enough to avoid simple one-move blunders most of the time, but haven't yet
developed the middlegame intuition to handle complex, dynamic positions"
(the "tactical awareness gap"). Data points reported from large
online-game analyses of this band:

- In complications, 1400–1600 players **lose ~0.8 pawns of advantage per
  move — nearly double the rate of 1700+ players**.
- Engines flag **an average of one significantly underactive piece** per
  middlegame in their games.
- In quiet positions, the player with the clearer plan wins only ~51% of
  the time in this band vs ~68% at 1700+ — planning skill exists but
  isn't yet a reliable weapon.

Openings are respectable, standard tactics are seen both ways, endgame
names are known. The difference from 1600+ is "fewer loose decisions
after the opening ends". GM Pert's 1000–1600 mistake themes (forcing-line
miscalculation, materialism, king safety) still apply at the band's
bottom; by its top the leak has visibly moved to planning and
complications.

What a typical game looks like: a theory-respecting opening into a known
structure; a middlegame where both sides have plans and the game turns on
one of three things — a complication one side handled a branch better, a
piece that never joined the game, or a structural concession made without
noticing. Endgames are real battlegrounds now; a tempo or an active rook
decides them.

## Rating context

1400–1600 chess.com rapid ≈ roughly 1300–1500 USCF, ≈ 1750–1950 Lichess
rapid. 1600 chess.com corresponds to a solid, established club player OTB
— the level where tournament sections stop being "novice".

## Depth of thought

Four to six ply, entering Steps 4 territory (preparatory-move tactics —
combinations that must be set up). Visualization now survives one quiet
move inside a line, but **branching is the new failure mode**: two
candidate replies get examined, the third — the refutation — doesn't.
Under time pressure the depth collapses back to two ply, which is where
the "0.8 pawns per move in complications" bleed comes from.

### The thought process to teach

Candidate discipline is assumed; what's added is **branch discipline**
and **verification**:

1. At the critical moment, name it out loud ("this is critical — a
   capture changes the structure / a sac is in the air").
2. Candidates ordered by forcingness; for the critical line, list *all*
   the opponent's plausible replies — the checklist question is "what's
   his worst-for-me answer?", not "what's his natural answer?"
3. Verify the critical line to quiescence; hold a **stepping-stone
   position** (a mental snapshot mid-line) and re-scan it fresh.
4. Budget: if the position is quiet, calculate less and plan more; if
   forcing, don't leave the line half-verified because the clock itches.

## Tactical ideas to teach

- **Full motif library completion**: interference, overloading chains
  (one defender, three jobs), desperado, counting refinements (the
  defender of the defender; pinned "defenders" that don't count).
- **Preparatory-move combinations** (Steps 4's theme): luring a piece to
  the fork square first, opening the line first, *then* the blow — 4–6
  ply with one setup move.
- **Defensive calculation as a first-class skill**: finding the only
  move, refuting unsound sacrifices confidently. This band declines
  winnable complications out of fear — the cure is a steady diet of
  "is the sac sound?" puzzles where half the answers are "no, take and
  hold".
- **Tactics *from* the structure**: which pawn breaks unleash which
  pieces (the d5 break in IQP positions as a tactical event; ...d5 in
  the Sicilian as the equalizing/counterattacking break).
- Calculation training proper: 2–3 positions per week solved *to
  written lines* without moving pieces, then checked — the Dojo-style
  "sweat work" habit starts here.

## Checkmates to know

The full named library is now assumed (Greek gift, smothered, Anastasia,
Arabian, Boden, Blackburne, hook, corridor, Damiano). New material is
**attack construction** rather than new mates:

- The standard kingside attack schemes: **h-file battery** after
  opposite castling (Qh-file + rook lift or h-pawn lever), **f6/g7
  wedge pawns** (a pawn on f6 makes Qg7# a permanent threat — many
  games win themselves after ...gxf6/gxf6 is forced), **rook lifts**
  (Re3–g3/h3 as the standard third attacker).
- **King hunts**: driving the king up the board with the net closing
  behind it — checks only when they shrink the king's box.
- **Perpetual check as a defensive resource** to hold worse positions
  (and the discipline to *look* for it before resigning mentally).
- Sacrifice bookkeeping: after any sac, count what's actually attacking
  vs defending — this band's attacks now fail from over-optimism more
  than under-counting.

## Openings

Repertoire consolidation at 8–10 moves in the critical lines, with the
emphasis moved to **model games**: for each pet line, a handful of master
games showing the standard middlegame plans.

- **Transposition awareness begins**: move orders reaching the same
  structure (London↔Slav shapes, Italian↔Giuoco slow shapes, Exchange
  QGD move orders) — think in *structures*, not move sequences.
- **The Sicilian becomes a reasonable project from ~1600** per multiple
  guides (and Sveshnikov's famous advice runs the other way — sharp open
  games teach; the point is choosing deliberately, not drifting).
- Sharp gambits get retired or made sound: the Evans stays if its
  middlegames are genuinely understood; junk traps go.
- The repertoire test for this band: **can you state the typical pawn
  break and the worst-placed enemy piece in your main line?** If not,
  the next opening hour goes there, not to a new variation.
- ~30% of study time on openings is now legitimate (ChessMood's
  1500–2000 split) — but "openings" means model games, plans and
  structures, not memorizing engine lines.

## Middlegame & strategic ideas

This band's growth area, per the data above:

- **Silman-style imbalance assessment** as a repeatable method (material,
  minor pieces, pawn structure, space, development, king safety,
  initiative): plans derive from imbalances, not moods. *How to Reassess
  Your Chess* becomes the textbook from here up.
- **The underactive-piece habit**: every few moves, name your worst
  piece and a route for it (directly targets the measured
  one-underactive-piece leak).
- **Prophylaxis introduced**: before your plan, ask "what does the
  opponent want?" and decide if it must be stopped. (Full prophylactic
  thinking is the 1600–1800 band's core; here it's one question.)
- **Tension management**: when to maintain, release or transform pawn
  tension — premature releases are a signature error here (every
  exchange "to simplify" hands the opponent a free file or tempo).
- Attack prerequisites enforced honestly; complications entered only
  with a concrete line, not a feeling — and declined only for a reason,
  not from fear.
- Space: what a space advantage actually obliges (avoid trades, prepare
  breaks on both wings) and how the cramped side frees (trade pieces,
  time the freeing break).

## Pawn structures

Structure family fluency becomes the plan generator:

- **Carlsbad**: execute the minority attack fully (b4–b5xc6, target c6
  with Rc1/Na4–c5/Qb3), and know Black's kingside/central counters
  (f5–f4 storms or the ...e5 break) and the timing race between them.
- **IQP both full plans**: attacker (piece activity, e5 knight, kingside
  pressure, the d5 break) and defender (blockade, simplification,
  endgame squeeze) — and the *isolani couple* transformation (d5xc6 →
  hanging pawns).
- **Hanging pawns**: strength while mobile (they gain space and open
  lines when they advance at the right moment), weakness when fixed;
  the c5/d5 advance decision as the position's hinge.
- **Chains**: correct lever choice and timing (against the French chain,
  ...c5 hits the base, ...f6 hits the head — which one depends on piece
  placement); locked center → wing play matched to the chain's
  direction.
- **Majority conversion technique**: candidate pawn first, no premature
  pushes, create the passer when it wins a tempo.
- Structural currencies: bishop pair vs structure damage, doubled pawns
  vs open file — trades read as transactions with prices.

## Typical combinations

- Multi-motif **4–6 ply combinations with one preparatory move** (the
  Steps 4 exam material).
- The **positional exchange sacrifice in embryo** (Rxc3-type ideas in
  Sicilian structures: wrecked pawns + dark squares for the exchange) —
  recognized, occasionally played, not yet a habit.
- **Pawn-storm sacrifices** against the castled king (h- and g-pawn
  levers given up to open files) with a defender-count justification.
- **Liquidating combinations**: forcing sequences that trade into a won
  Silman-Class-C/B ending *on purpose* — the most reliable winning
  method available to the band.
- Refutation combinations: the tactical sequence that punishes the
  opponent's unsound sac (calculated acceptance as a skill).

## Endgames

Silman Class C completed, Class B (1600–1799) begun:

- **Lucena and Philidor beyond recognition to execution under clock
  pressure**, including the defender's *choices* (when Philidor's
  passive rook fails and the rook must go active behind the pawn).
- **Triangulation and outflanking** (Class B's king-and-pawn upgrades):
  losing a tempo with the king to put the opponent in zugzwang;
  approaching diagonally to steal the opposition.
- **Rook + two connected pawns vs rook**; the **7th-rank rook** (worth a
  pawn in itself); **two bishops vs king** mate as a technique rite.
- **Opposite-colored bishops deeper**: when the extra pawn wins (two
  connected passers far apart, king penetration) and when it never does
  (single passer, blockade on the bishop's color).
- **Q vs 7th-rank pawn** all four cases automatic.
- Practical rook-endgame law drilled: **activity over material** — the
  active rook is worth a pawn; passive defense loses drawn positions.
- The transition habit: before *any* mass liquidation, name the ending
  you're entering and its theoretical result.

## Time management & practical play

- 30+ minute games become the norm for serious play (the band's
  calculation depth doesn't fit in less); 15+10 as the floor.
- **Complication budgeting**: entering a forcing sequence is a spending
  decision — the rule "once in, every move gets verified" prevents the
  measured mid-line collapse. If the verification budget isn't there,
  choose the quiet continuation.
- Time-trouble policy: at <5 minutes, switch to forcing-move-first,
  safety-first play deliberately (a known mode, not a panic).
- Psychology enters formally: the second error follows the first within
  three moves at every level below master — the post-mistake ritual
  (breathe, reassess material and threats coldly, play one solid move)
  is teachable now.

## Typical blunders (what the coach watches for)

1. Eval bleed in complications (the 0.8-pawns-per-move problem):
   entering forcing sequences with unverified branches.
2. The underactive piece left out of the game for 15 moves.
3. "Active-looking" moves with no target (plan drift measured against
   the position's demands).
4. Premature pawn-tension release; wrong-wing play with an open center.
5. Winning positions over-pressed into losses; drawing techniques
   unknown when worse (perpetual/fortress never sought).
6. Deep-line miscalculation where a quiet in-between move refutes.
7. Fear-declines: winnable complications avoided without calculating
   them (the mirror image of #1).

## A study week at 1400–1600

Migrating from 70/15/15 toward 50/30/10/10:

- **Calculation/tactics (~50%)**: daily puzzles at accuracy pace, plus
  2–3 "sweat work" positions per week solved to written lines without
  moving pieces; a weekly block of defensive/only-move puzzles.
- **Openings (~30%)**: model games in your structures (2–3 per week,
  annotated lightly: "what was the pawn break? which piece was traded
  and why?"); patch own-game holes the same week they appear.
- **Middlegame (~10%)**: imbalance write-ups on 1–2 quiet positions
  from your own games; one Amateur's Mind / HTRYC chapter section.
- **Endgame (~10%)**: Class B items as sparring drills (triangulation
  positions, R+2 vs R vs the engine, OCB pawn-up tries).
- Play 3–4 slow games (at least one 30+); annotate one deeply; review
  every complication from your games move by move — re-calculate the
  branches on the board (the single highest-yield exercise for the
  band's measured leak).

## What a good teacher does here

Shifts training weight from puzzles toward **middlegame study and
own-game complications review**: every complication from the student's
games gets re-calculated on the board, branch by branch. Teaches the
imbalance assessment out loud until it's automatic; assigns model games
per opening; runs structured calculation training (candidates → order by
forcingness → verify the critical line to quiescence). Longer time
controls become the norm. Feedback compares the student's plan against
the position's demanded plan — "your idea was fine in a closed center;
here the center was open".

## Graduation checklist (ready for 1600–1800 when…)

- [ ] Complication bleed visibly down: reviewed forcing sequences show
      verified branches (spot-check three complications from recent
      games).
- [ ] No piece sits underactive for 10+ moves in reviewed games; the
      worst-piece question is asked unprompted.
- [ ] Preparatory-move combinations (~1600–1700 puzzle level) at ~85%;
      defensive/only-move puzzles no longer scored worse than attacking
      ones.
- [ ] Triangulation and outflanking demonstrated against the engine;
      R+2 vs R converted; activity-over-material narrated in a rook
      ending from own play.
- [ ] For each repertoire branch: typical pawn break + worst enemy piece
      stated from memory; model game named.
- [ ] Quiet-position plans win their share: reviewed quiet middlegames
      show a nameable, position-appropriate plan most of the time.

## Coach tuning hints (our app)

- Explanations: 5–6 ply; positional vocabulary open (prophylaxis,
  tension, blockade) — define once per session.
- The coach's highest-value observations here, per the data: name the
  underactive piece; flag entry into complications ("from here, every
  move must be forcing and verified"); critique plans against the
  structure by name ("in the Carlsbad, your play is b4–b5, not h4").
- Engine deltas natural; show the refutation branch when a line was
  miscalculated, not just the better first move.
- Praise correct *decisions* (keeping tension, completing development
  before striking, declining a bad complication with a reason), not
  only correct moves.
- When the player is worse, surface drawing-resource prompts
  (perpetual/fortress/opposite-bishops) — the band systematically
  forgets these exist.
