# 400–600 · Absolute beginner

[← index](README.md) · [next band →](elo-0600-0800.md)

## Player expectations

Knows how the pieces move, often shakily on the special rules (castling
conditions, en passant, stalemate vs checkmate). Community analysis of
games at this level is consistent: nearly every game contains five or more
blunders on each side, and the result is decided by who hangs the *last*
piece, not the first. A typical 500-rated game swings from completely
winning to completely lost several times: a queen traded for a rook, a full
piece left en prise, a knight fork on king and rook, a missed mate in one.
Players push wing pawns (a4/h4) while minor pieces sit at home, bring the
queen out on move 3–4, and frequently fail to *capture* pieces their
opponent has hung.

## Depth of thought

Zero to one ply reliably. The player sees the move they want to make and
its immediate purpose; the opponent's reply is not part of the picture yet
(Heisman calls this "hope chess"). Board vision itself is the bottleneck —
they don't yet *see* all attacked squares, so "calculate more" is
meaningless advice. Steps Method Step 1 exercises (attack/capture, mate in
one) are exactly one ply for this reason.

## Tactical ideas to teach

- **Counting on one square**: is the piece I want to take defended? Is my
  piece attacked more times than it is defended?
- **Free pieces**: take hanging pieces (this must be taught explicitly —
  players at this level routinely don't); don't leave your own hanging.
- **Piece values** (1/3/3/5/9) as the tool for judging every trade.
- **Mate in one**: find it for yourself; see it coming against you.
- Twofold attack in its simplest form ("one move, two jobs").

## Checkmates to know

- Ladder mate (two rooks / rook + queen).
- King + Queen vs king — with explicit stalemate awareness (the classic
  failure at this level is stalemating with a huge material lead).
- King + Rook vs king (the "box"), slowly.
- "Overkill mates" (Silman's beginner chapter): two queens, queen + rook.
- Recognize the **Scholar's mate threat** (Qh5/Qf3 + Bc4 hitting f7) and
  the standard defenses (g6/Nf6, develop and hit the queen).

## Openings

No theory whatsoever. Three principles, repeated every game: put a pawn in
the center, develop knights and bishops toward the center, castle early.
Negative rules matter as much: don't bring the queen out early, don't push
edge pawns, don't move the same piece twice without reason, don't grab
pawns with the queen. A "repertoire" here is 1.e4 + "develop and castle".

## Middlegame & strategic ideas

Strategy *is* piece safety at this level: keep pieces defended, take free
material, trade only when the count favors you, and when ahead trade
pieces (not pawns) and promote something. "Improve your worst piece" can
be introduced as "is anyone still asleep at home?".

## Pawn structures

Only two ideas: keep the three pawns in front of your castled king at home,
and connected pawns defend each other. No named structures.

## Typical combinations

None expected. The whole of combinative play at this level is: check that
forces the king to move, then take the hanging piece — and the knight fork
on king + rook (usually suffered rather than played; teach the e2/e7 and
c2/c7 fork squares defensively).

## Endgames

Silman (unrated–999): the overkill mates above, K+Q vs K, K+R vs K,
stalemate tricks and how to avoid giving them. Plus the practical rule that
decides most games here: push your extra pawns, make a new queen, then mate
with overwhelming force.

## Typical blunders (what the coach watches for)

1. Leaving a piece en prise (the single dominant game-decider).
2. Not capturing an opponent's hanging piece.
3. Missing mate-in-1 threats against their own king.
4. Stalemate with a winning position; rushed "checking for checking's sake".
5. Losing the queen to early adventures; queen-for-rook trades.
6. f7/f2 collapses (Scholar's-type attacks).

## What a good teacher does here

One habit above everything: the **safety check** — before each move, "is my
move safe? does their reply attack anything?" One lesson per game, warmly
delivered; the enemy at this level is discouragement, not ignorance. Slow
time controls only. No opening study, no strategy lectures, no variations.
Celebrate every game where nothing was hung, regardless of result.

## Coach tuning hints (our app)

- Threat arrow + hanging-piece warnings are the loudest outputs; almost
  every coach message should answer "is anything hanging, mine or theirs?"
- Explanations: one move per side, plain words (attack, defend, free,
  safe); no motif names, no evaluations in pawns ("you can win a knight
  here" instead of "+3.2").
- Recommendation window is at its widest — any safe developing move within
  ~3 pawns of best is fine to praise.
- Review: flag only large errors (≥ ~1.5 pawns); one comment per move
  maximum; praise safe moves explicitly.
