## Player expectations

Knows how the pieces move, often shakily on the special rules (castling
conditions, en passant, promotion choices, stalemate vs checkmate).
Community analysis of games at this level is consistent: nearly every game
contains five or more blunders per side, and the result is decided by who
hangs the *last* piece, not the first. A typical 500-rated game swings from
completely winning to completely lost several times: a queen traded for a
rook, a full piece left en prise for three consecutive moves (with neither
player noticing), a knight fork on king and rook, a missed mate in one.

What a typical game looks like: 1.e4 e5, a couple of natural developing
moves, then a wing-pawn push (a4/h4) or an early queen sortie; somewhere
between moves 6 and 15 a piece is left hanging and *not taken* for a move
or two; the first player to consolidate an extra queen wins, frequently
after stalemate scares. Players at this level also routinely fail to
*capture* pieces their opponent has hung — board vision misses free
material in both directions, which is the single most important fact about
the band.

Forum threads comparing 200–800 play agree on the discriminator: at 400,
pieces hang unprompted; by 600, they hang mainly under light pressure; by
800, mainly under real tactical pressure. Progress through this band *is*
progress in noticing.

## Rating context

Online and over-the-board scales diverge most at the bottom. A 500
chess.com rapid player would typically sit below most published OTB rating
floors (USCF's floor is 100; new scholastic players often start in the
100–400 range). Lichess ratings for the same player run substantially
higher (Lichess starts everyone at 1500 and its scale sits above
chess.com's; the gap stretches at the low end — a 500 chess.com player
often shows 900–1100 on Lichess). When the coach mentions ratings it should
name the scale.

## Depth of thought

Zero to one ply reliably. The player sees the move they want to make and
its immediate purpose; the opponent's reply is not part of the picture yet.
Heisman's classification is exact here: this is "Hope Chess" — moves are
made without asking whether the opponent's checks, captures or threats in
reply can be met. The bottleneck is not calculation but **board vision**:
the player does not yet *see* all attacked squares, so "calculate more" is
meaningless advice. Steps Method Step 1 exercises (attack/capture, mate in
one) are exactly one ply deep for this reason.

### The thought process to teach

One question, asked before every single move, out loud at first:

> **"Is my move safe? After I play it, can anything of mine be taken?"**

And its twin on the opponent's turn: **"Did their move leave anything free
for me?"** Nothing more. A two-item checklist survives contact with a real
game; a seven-item checklist does not. The documented drill for this band:
play full games where the only goal is *keeping every piece defended* —
scan all your pieces each move; result of the game irrelevant. Coaches
report this single exercise ("undefended piece awareness") moves players
out of the 400s faster than any content.

## Tactical ideas to teach

- **Counting on one square**: is the piece I want to take defended? Am I
  attacked more times than I am defended? One square at a time — full
  exchange sequences are a later band's material.
- **Free pieces**: take hanging pieces (this must be taught explicitly —
  players at this level routinely don't); don't leave your own hanging.
- **Piece values** (Q=9, R=5, B=3, N=3, P=1) as the tool for judging every
  trade; "don't trade a bigger number for a smaller one without a reason
  you can say out loud."
- **Mate in one**: find it for yourself; see it coming against you. This is
  the entire puzzle curriculum for the band, and it doubles as board-vision
  training (which squares does each piece control?).
- **Twofold attack in its simplest form** ("one move, two jobs") — noticing
  it, not yet engineering it.
- Defensive awareness of the knight: the knight is the piece this band
  loses material to, because its moves are the hardest to visualize. A
  drill that pays immediately: name every square the opponent's knight can
  reach next move.

## Checkmates to know

- **Ladder mate** (two rooks or rook + queen, driving the king rank by
  rank). The default win at this level; teach it as "the two pieces take
  turns; never let your rook get close enough to be touched by the king."
- **King + Queen vs king** — the queen boxes the king to the edge a knight's
  move away, the king walks up, mate delivered with king support. Taught
  with explicit **stalemate alarms**: the classic failure of the band is
  stalemating with a queen and two rooks extra. Rule of thumb to teach:
  when the enemy king is alone, before every move ask "if they can't move,
  is it check?".
- **King + Rook vs king** (the box method), slowly, as a stretch goal.
- **Overkill mates** (Silman's beginner chapter): two queens, queen + rook,
  two rooks — how to finish quickly *without* stalemate.
- **Scholar's mate — defense first**: recognize the Qh5/Qf3 + Bc4 battery
  aimed at f7, and the standard cures (2...Nc6 then 3...g6 against Qh5, or
  ...Nf6 lines against Qf3; develop while hitting the queen). Players in
  this band lose to it repeatedly until shown; teaching the defense also
  quietly teaches why f7/f2 are the softest squares on the board.

## Openings

No theory whatsoever — reputable beginner guides are unanimous. Three
principles, repeated every game until they are boring:

1. Put a pawn in the center (1.e4 or 1.d4; answer 1.e4 with 1...e5,
   1.d4 with 1...d5).
2. Develop knights and bishops toward the center — knights before bishops
   is a decent default; aim for all four minor pieces off the back rank by
   move 8.
3. Castle early (kingside), then connect the rooks.

Negative rules matter as much as positive ones at this level: don't bring
the queen out before minor pieces, don't push edge pawns, don't move the
same piece twice in the opening without a spoken reason, don't grab pawns
with the queen, don't open the f-file with your king uncastled. A
"repertoire" here is 1.e4 plus "develop and castle" — nothing more. The
only "theory" worth a beginner's minutes is trap *defense*: Scholar's mate
(above) and not touching the f7/f2 pawn complex carelessly.

## Middlegame & strategic ideas

Strategy *is* piece safety at this level. Everything else is decoration:

- Keep pieces defended; take free material; trade only when the count and
  the values favor you.
- When ahead in material: **trade pieces, not pawns**, then promote
  something. When behind: keep queens on (taught later; here it's enough
  that "ahead → trade" sticks).
- "Improve your worst piece" can be introduced in child form: "is anyone
  still asleep at home?"
- Attack nothing with one piece alone; invite a friend first.

## Pawn structures

Only two ideas, both framed as safety:

- Keep the three pawns in front of your castled king at home (h2/g2/f2
  untouched unless forced).
- Connected pawns defend each other; a pawn that steps forward can never
  step back.

No named structures. Doubled/isolated pawns are not yet vocabulary — the
cost/benefit is invisible below ~800 and the words crowd out the safety
habit.

## Typical combinations

None expected, and none should be taught as "combinations". The whole of
combinative play at this level reduces to two shapes:

1. **Check, then take**: a check that forces the king to move, after which
   a hanging piece falls. (One forcing move + one capture — exactly two
   ply, the band's ceiling.)
2. **The knight fork on king and rook** — usually suffered rather than
   played. Teach it defensively: the squares c2/c7 and f2/f7 (and e2/e7
   early on) are where enemy knights fork; notice when a knight is one hop
   away.

## Endgames

Silman's Beginner class (unrated–999) is the exact syllabus:

- The overkill mates (two queens; queen + rook; two rooks — the staircase).
- K+Q vs K; K+R vs K (the box).
- **Stalemate alert**: recognize the three classic stalemate shapes with
  the bare king in the corner; the habit "check their legal moves before
  every quiet move in a won ending".

Plus the practical rule that decides most games here: push your extra
pawns, make a new queen, then mate with overwhelming force. Pawn endings
proper (opposition, key squares) are *not* this band's material — a bare
"walk your king in front of your passed pawn" is as far as it goes.

## Time management & practical play

- Play 15+10 or slower, and *use* the clock: the documented failure is
  moving instantly, not flagging. A concrete pacing rule: after every
  opponent move, sit on your hands until you have answered "what did that
  move attack?"
- One long game with full attention beats five careless ones; guides for
  this band consistently prescribe rapid over blitz because blitz hides
  the exact skill (noticing) the band needs to build.
- Resign nothing: at this level no material deficit is decisive, and
  playing on trains the mop-up and stalemate skills the band lacks.

## Typical blunders (what the coach watches for)

1. Leaving a piece en prise (the single dominant game-decider).
2. Not capturing an opponent's hanging piece.
3. Missing mate-in-1 threats against their own king.
4. Stalemate with a winning position; "checking for checking's sake".
5. Losing the queen to early adventures; queen-for-rook (or worse) trades.
6. f7/f2 collapses (Scholar's-type attacks).
7. Castling rights thrown away by careless king/rook touches, then a king
   stuck in the center.

## A study week at 400–600

Improvement literature for this band converges on "play slowly and look":

- 4–5 rapid games (15+10), each with the safety-question habit enforced.
- After each game, 5 minutes: find every hung piece (yours and theirs)
  with the analysis board — count them; the count trending down *is* the
  rating graph.
- 10 minutes/day of mate-in-1 and "take the free piece" puzzles, accuracy
  over speed.
- One K+Q vs K and one ladder-mate execution against an engine or a friend
  per week until both are boring.
- Nothing else. No opening videos, no strategy books, no motif lists.

## What a good teacher does here

One habit above everything: the **safety check** — before each move, "is my
move safe? does their reply attack anything?" One lesson per game review,
warmly delivered; the enemy at this level is discouragement, not ignorance.
Slow time controls only. No opening study, no strategy lectures, no
variations. Celebrate every game where nothing was hung, regardless of
result — that is the metric that matters, and the player should know it.

## Graduation checklist (ready for 600–800 when…)

- [ ] Fewer than two hanging-piece incidents per game (either direction),
      measured over ~10 games.
- [ ] Mate-in-1 puzzles ~95% accurate without time pressure.
- [ ] K+Q vs K and ladder mate executed without stalemate, every time.
- [ ] Castles by move ~10 in most games; queen stays home until minors are
      out.
- [ ] Can answer "what did their last move attack?" on demand.

## Coach tuning hints (our app)

- Threat arrow + hanging-piece warnings are the loudest outputs; almost
  every coach message should answer "is anything hanging, mine or theirs?"
- Explanations: one move per side, plain words (attack, defend, free,
  safe); no motif names, no evaluations in pawns ("you can win a knight
  here" instead of "+3.2").
- Recommendation window is at its widest — any safe developing move within
  ~3 pawns of best is fine to praise.
- Review: flag only large errors (≥ ~1.5 pawns); one comment per move
  maximum; praise safe moves explicitly ("nothing hanging after this —
  good").
- Stalemate guard: in K+Q(+more) vs K endings, warn before any move that
  leaves the defender without legal moves.
