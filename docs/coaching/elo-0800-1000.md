## Player expectations

The transition band from "beginner" to "improving amateur". Coaching
literature marks 800–1000 as the range where players *begin to spot mates
in 1 and 2 when they appear in their own games* — the first sign of pattern
recognition working under game conditions, not just in puzzles. By 1000:
simple one-move threats are usually detected, knight forks and back-rank
ideas start appearing in the player's **own plans** (not just against
them), and mate-in-1 is essentially never missed with time on the clock.

Persistent weaknesses reported for the band: non-knight double attacks
(bishop, rook and especially queen forks are watched far less carefully
than knight forks), the in-between move, endgames beyond basic mates, and
time management. Games still swing, but a clean win without a major
blunder is now possible and worth celebrating when it happens.

What a typical game looks like: a sensible opening into a playable
middlegame; around move 15–25 one side wins material with a two-move
tactic the other side "knew" but didn't check; conversion is wobbly — the
material leader often lets the game re-complicate once or twice before the
point lands. Draws are still rare; someone usually falls over.

## Rating context

800–1000 chess.com rapid ≈ roughly 600–900 USCF OTB, ≈ 1150–1400 on
Lichess rapid. This is also where the population gets dense: reaching
~1000 on chess.com puts a player at about the median of *active* players —
useful framing for morale ("average" is an achievement from below).

## Depth of thought

Two ply reliably, three in forced capture sequences — matching the upper
Steps 2 exercises (2–4 ply). The player can follow "I take, they take, I
take" but loses the thread the moment a reply is *not* a recapture: the
in-between move (**zwischenzug**) is the invisible enemy of this band, on
both sides of the board. Steps 3's "thinking ahead" skill — holding a
changed position in the mind's eye for one extra move — is the growth
edge, and it is trainable with visualization-flavored puzzles (solve
mate-in-2 *without moving the pieces*).

### The thought process to teach

The scan matures into a fixed order, still short:

1. **What did their move change?** (new attacks, new undefended pieces,
   opened lines — theirs *and* mine.)
2. **My checks, captures, threats** — list them before liking any of them.
3. **Pick a candidate → blunder-check it**: "after my move, what is their
   best check, capture or threat?"

Step 3 is the one that decays under excitement; reviews should audit it
("show me where you blunder-checked this move").

## Tactical ideas to teach

- The **full basic motif set, in both directions** (for me / against me):
  fork with *every* piece (queen forks especially — check + loose piece
  is the queen's bread and butter), pin (absolute vs relative), skewer,
  discovered attack, double attack, back rank.
- **Removing the defender**, introduced as a family: capture the guard,
  chase it, or overload it — then take what it was guarding.
- **Mate in 2 as the standard puzzle diet**, plus counting exercises with
  3+ attackers/defenders on one square (order of captures matters:
  cheapest first; recount after each pair).
- **Why forcing moves are searched first**: checks restrict replies to a
  countable set — the first taste of real calculation method.
- **Discovered check** as a special weapon ("the moving piece is
  invulnerable this move — it can take anything").
- Zwischenzug, defensively: "before any recapture — check their checks."

## Checkmates to know

- **Back-rank combinations with one deflection** ("remove the guard, then
  mate"): Rd8+-style trades that drag the defender away, queen sacs on
  the back rank that force rook recaptures into mate.
- **Smothered mate — the full Philidor's Legacy** as an aspirational
  pattern: Qc4+/Qb3+ geometry, Nf7+, Nh6++ (double check!), Qg8+!! Rxg8,
  Nf7#. Teaches three ideas at once: double check, forced replies, and
  the queen sacrifice as a *tool*.
- **Anastasia's mate** (knight on e7 seals g8/g6; rook mates on the
  h-file, often after a Qxh7+! decoy) and **Arabian mate** (Nf6 + Rh7/Rh8
  in the corner) as recognized shapes.
- **Corridor mates** generalized from the back rank (any rank/file the
  king can't leave).
- **Q+B and Q+N batteries against h7/h2**: count defenders before
  "attacking"; recognize when the battery is real (Ng5+Qh5 vs h7 with
  only the king defending).
- Defensive homework: create **luft before** the back rank becomes hot;
  notice when your own pieces smother your king.

## Openings

Pick a fixed trio and repeat it for months:

- **White**: one scheme. The **Italian** (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4, with
  c3+d4 or the slow d3 plan) is the recommended default for its open,
  tactical teaching value. The **London** (d4, Nf3, Bf4, e3, Bd3, Nbd2,
  c3) is acceptable for a structure-first temperament — with the warning,
  echoed by multiple coaches, that autopilot systems can stunt tactical
  growth if they replace thinking.
- **Vs 1.e4**: 1...e5 (recommended — the resulting open games teach the
  most) or the **Caro-Kann** (1...c6 + 2...d5) for reliability.
- **Vs 1.d4**: 1...d5 with simple development (the Queen's Gambit
  Declined setup e6/Nf6/Be7/O-O works against nearly everything).

Depth: three to five moves plus the *ideas* — sub-1200 repertoire guides
agree that more theory than this dilutes into confusion. Trap literacy
grows: the Fried Liver **defense** now learned properly (after 3...Nf6
4.Ng5 d5 5.exd5, play 5...Na5 — not 5...Nxd5), Fishing Pole awareness
(don't grab the "free" knight on g4/g5 next to your castled king), and
never falling for early Qh5 systems again.

## Middlegame & strategic ideas

- First real plans, stated as recipes: **"improve your worst piece"**,
  rooks to open/half-open files, the three-piece kingside attack recipe
  (queen + two minors aimed at h7/f7 — and the honest count of
  defenders before starting).
- **Trade logic sharpened**: ahead in material → trade pieces not pawns;
  never initiate trades that only activate the opponent (the recapture
  develops them); trades that straighten *their* pawns need a reason.
- **Targets**: f7/f2 before castling, h7/h2 after; the weak back rank as
  a plan (stack heavy pieces before the mate exists — it will appear).
- Count attackers vs defenders *before* starting any attack; abandon
  attacks the count rejects (this band's attacks are two pieces vs a
  defended king far too often).
- The first "don't": don't launch pawns in front of your own king to
  attack — the holes stay.

## Pawn structures

Vocabulary becomes real, still practical:

- **Doubled, isolated, backward** pawns and *why* they're weak: they can't
  be defended by pawns, so pieces get tied to them. (And the first
  counterexample: doubled pawns toward the center that open a file for a
  rook are often fine.)
- **Passed pawn** as an endgame asset — created by trades, escorted by
  the king.
- Don't open the h-file in front of your own castled king; recapture
  toward the center refined with exceptions ("...unless it opens a file
  for your rook or passes a pawn").
- First sight of the **pawn majority** idea: 3 vs 2 on one side can make
  a passer; crippled majorities (doubled) can't.

## Typical combinations

Two-movers with a forcing first move, plus the band's first three-mover:

1. **Deflect the back-rank defender, then mate** (Qxd8/Rxd8 tricks —
   check whether the recapturing piece abandoned the mate square).
2. **Check that drags the king onto a fork square** (Q or N picks up the
   loose piece next move).
3. **Pin, then pile up** on the pinned piece (add attackers faster than
   they add defenders; the pawn push against a pinned knight).
4. **Discovered check that wins material**: the front piece grabs a rook
   or queen while the check freezes the reply.
5. Top of band, the first three-mover: **sacrifice on f7**, king takes,
   knight/queen check regains the material with interest (the Fried
   Liver shape, now played rather than suffered).

## Endgames

Silman Class E (1000–1199), now in earnest:

- **What material mates a lone king** and what cannot (K+2N cannot force
  it; K+B / K+N alone never) — now used prospectively when choosing
  trades ("this trade leaves me unable to win").
- **Minor piece vs queen**: why the queen wins (forks, boxing), and how
  the defender makes it hardest (piece near king, no loose squares).
- **The opposition**, introduced as *the* king-and-pawn concept: kings
  facing with one square between, the player *not* to move has the
  opposition; with it, the attacking king forces its way forward.
- **Rook-pawn special cases**: the drawing corner (defender reaches the
  corner → draw even a rook-pawn up); wrong-bishop awareness previewed.
- **Square of the pawn** to settle races without move-counting.
- **Use your king**: once queens are off, the king marches — centralize
  it before pushing pawns.

## Time management & practical play

- 15+10 stays the staple; add an occasional 30-minute game to practice
  sustained attention (this is also where scholastic players first meet
  OTB classical).
- Budget rule of thumb: opening on memory+principles (fast), first
  contact and all captures (slow), mop-up (fast but blunder-checked).
- The band's classic clock failure: burning minutes early on quiet moves,
  then blitzing the critical recapture. Reviews should point at the
  *timestamps*, not just the moves.
- Losses on time in winning positions → prescribe more increment, not
  faster play.

## Typical blunders (what the coach watches for)

1. Bishop/rook/queen double attacks missed (knight forks are now watched;
   the others aren't).
2. Zwischenzug victims — assuming every capture gets an automatic
   recapture and finding the queen gone two moves later.
3. Pawn races miscounted; kings left out of endgames entirely.
4. Back rank while all heavy pieces left home; luft never made under
   "attacking excitement".
5. Attacks launched with two pieces against a three-defender king.
6. Clock burned early, then panic blunders in winning positions.
7. Trades into unwinnable material (K+N vs K "wins" that are draws).

## A study week at 800–1000

- 4–5 games of 15+10 (one 30-minute game if possible), each reviewed for
  ~10 minutes: first pass *without* engine ("where did it turn?"), then
  engine check of the two biggest moments.
- 20–30 puzzles/day from a **restricted motif menu** (this week: pins +
  discovered attacks; next week: queen forks + back rank), accuracy over
  speed; every miss replayed until seen.
- One new motif introduced per week with 15–20 dedicated puzzles.
- Endgame: 2 sessions/week, 15 minutes — opposition drills (K+P vs K from
  both sides against the engine), square-of-the-pawn positions, Q vs
  minor piece.
- Annotate **one own game per week in plain sentences** ("I thought…, his
  move surprised me because…") — the habit that pays compound interest
  for the rest of the ladder.

## What a good teacher does here

Consolidates the threat-first habit (it exists now, but decays under
excitement); makes the student announce the opponent's threat out loud
before choosing a move in reviews. Starts endgame lessons in earnest —
they pay immediately at this level because opponents can't convert.
Introduces one new motif at a time with a puzzle block on it. Insists on
rapid (15+10) over blitz; assigns the weekly annotated game. Praise
targets *process* ("you checked his reply before moving — that's why this
move is good") to cement the checklist.

## Graduation checklist (ready for 1000–1200 when…)

- [ ] Mate-in-2 puzzles ~85%+; mate-in-1 never missed in games.
- [ ] Queen/bishop/rook forks spotted as reliably as knight forks
      (puzzle-verified).
- [ ] Opposition used correctly in K+P vs K, both sides, against the
      engine.
- [ ] The blunder-check ("their best check/capture/threat after my
      move") is narratable for most moves of a reviewed game.
- [ ] No more than one two-move-tactic loss per ~5 games.
- [ ] The fixed opening trio played in ≥80% of games for a month.

## Coach tuning hints (our app)

- Explanations: 2–3 ply; bare motif names fine (fork, pin, skewer,
  discovered attack); still no centipawn talk — use "wins a knight",
  "loses a pawn".
- Always report *both* sides' hanging pieces and the opponent's best
  threat; call out fork geometries (all pieces, not just knights) and
  back-rank status when relevant.
- Endgame positions: name the concept in play (opposition, square of the
  pawn, drawing corner) — this band gains the most from endgame naming.
- Review cadence: one main comment per move, plus praise when the
  player's move matches a known motif ("that's a discovered attack —
  well seen").
- On opponent captures, keep the zwischenzug guard: "any check better
  than recapturing?"
