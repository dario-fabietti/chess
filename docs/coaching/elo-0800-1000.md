# 800–1000 · Advancing beginner

[← 600–800](elo-0600-0800.md) · [index](README.md) · [next band →](elo-1000-1200.md)

## Player expectations

The transition band from "beginner" to "improving amateur". Coaching
literature marks 800–1000 as the range where players *begin to spot mates
in 1 and 2 when they appear in their own games* — the first sign of pattern
recognition working under game conditions. By 1000: simple one-move threats
are usually detected, knight forks and back-rank ideas start appearing in
the player's **own plans** (not just against them), and mate-in-1 puzzles
are solved consistently. Persistent weaknesses reported for the band:
non-knight forks (bishop/rook/queen double attacks), endgames, and time
management. Games still swing, but a clean win without a major blunder is
now possible.

## Depth of thought

Two ply reliably, three in forced capture sequences. This matches the lower
Steps 2 exercises (2–4 ply). The player can follow "I take, they take, I
take" but loses the thread the moment a reply is *not* a recapture — the
in-between move (zwischenzug) is the invisible enemy of this band.

## Tactical ideas to teach

- The full basic motif set, now in **both directions** (for me / against
  me): fork (all pieces, not just knights), pin, skewer, discovered attack,
  back rank, double attack.
- **Removing the defender** introduced: capture or chase the one piece
  guarding against mate or material loss.
- Mate in 2 as the standard puzzle diet; counting multi-piece exchanges.
- First taste of *why* forcing moves are searched first: checks restrict
  replies.

## Checkmates to know

- Back-rank combinations with one deflection ("remove the guard, then
  mate").
- **Smothered mate** — the full Philidor's Legacy sequence (Qg8+!! then
  Nf7#) as a motivating pattern to aspire to.
- Anastasia's mate and Arabian mate shapes (knight + rook geometry).
- Corridor mates; Q+B / Q+N batteries against h7/h2.
- Defensive homework: create luft *before* the back rank becomes hot.

## Openings

Pick a fixed trio and repeat it: one White scheme (Italian recommended for
its open, tactical teaching value; London acceptable for the
structure-first temperament), one answer to 1.e4 (1...e5 or the Caro-Kann
for reliability), one answer to 1.d4 (1...d5). Three to five moves deep
plus the *ideas* — repertoire guides for sub-1200 players agree that more
theory than this dilutes into confusion. Trap literacy grows: Fried Liver
defense, Fishing Pole awareness, not falling for early Qh5 systems.

## Middlegame & strategic ideas

- First real plans: "improve your worst piece", rooks to open/half-open
  files, simple three-piece kingside attack recipe.
- Trade logic sharpened: ahead in material → trade pieces not pawns;
  never initiate trades that only activate the opponent.
- Targets: f7/f2 before castling, h7/h2 after; weak back rank as a plan.
- Count attackers vs defenders *before* starting any attack.

## Pawn structures

Vocabulary becomes real: doubled, isolated, backward pawns and *why* they
are weak (can't be defended by pawns); passed pawn as an endgame asset;
don't open the h-file in front of your own castled king. Capture-toward-
the-center refined with exceptions.

## Typical combinations

Two-movers with a forcing first move: deflect the back-rank defender then
mate; check that drags the king onto a fork; pin then pile up on the pinned
piece. At the top of the band, the first three-move combination: sacrifice
on f7, king takes, knight/queen check regains material with interest.

## Endgames

Silman Class E (1001–1199), now in earnest: which material mates a lone
king and which cannot; minor piece vs queen (why the queen wins); **the
opposition** introduced as the key king-and-pawn concept; rook-pawn special
cases (the drawing corner); square of the pawn to settle races without
counting move by move.

## Typical blunders (what the coach watches for)

1. Bishop/rook/queen double attacks missed (knight forks are now watched;
   the others aren't).
2. Zwischenzug victims — assuming every capture gets an automatic
   recapture.
3. Pawn races miscounted; kings left out of endgames entirely.
4. Back rank while all rooks left home; luft never made.
5. Attacks launched with two pieces against a defended king.
6. Clock burned early, then panic blunders in winning positions.

## What a good teacher does here

Consolidates the threat-first habit (it exists now, but decays under
excitement); makes the student announce the opponent's threat out loud
before choosing a move in reviews. Starts endgame lessons in earnest —
they pay immediately at this level because opponents can't convert.
Introduces one new motif at a time with 15–20 puzzles on it. Insists on
rapid (15+10) over blitz; assigns annotating one own game per week in
plain sentences.

## Coach tuning hints (our app)

- Explanations: 2–3 ply; bare motif names fine (fork, pin, skewer,
  discovered attack); still no centipawn talk — use "wins a knight",
  "loses a pawn".
- Always report *both* sides' hanging pieces and the opponent's best
  threat; call out fork geometries and back-rank status when relevant.
- Endgame positions: name the concept in play (opposition, square of the
  pawn) — this band gains the most from endgame naming.
- Review cadence: one main comment per move, plus praise when the player's
  move matches a known motif ("that's a discovered attack — well seen").
