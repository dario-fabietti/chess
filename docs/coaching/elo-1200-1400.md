## Player expectations

Rating guides describe this band as playing "chess in the real sense":
one-move blunders are now the exception; a small personal repertoire
exists with theory up to ~move 8–10 in the pet lines; Lucena and Philidor
are recognized *by name*; tactical vision reaches four-move combinations
on a good day. GM Nick Pert's "typical mistakes" bracket for 1000–1600
identifies the band's signature errors precisely: **miscalculating forcing
lines, being too materialistic, and king-safety negligence**.

Losses come from two- to three-move tactics, from strategic drift after
the opening ends ("I developed… now what?"), and from technique — winning
positions that don't get won. This is also where "hope chess" finally
dies: the player asks what the opponent's move threatens on most moves,
not just some.

What a typical game looks like: a real opening battle for 10 moves; a
middlegame where both sides have *ideas* (an attack, an open file) but
execute them with tactical leaks; the exchange or a couple of pawns
change hands mid-game; and an endgame that is now sometimes *played*
rather than stumbled through — with the stronger technique winning drawn
positions and drawing won ones.

## Rating context

1200–1400 chess.com rapid ≈ roughly 1100–1300 USCF, ≈ 1550–1750 Lichess
rapid. This is the median band of most OTB club rooms' lower boards, and
the level internet culture calls "intermediate" without irony.

## Depth of thought

Three to five ply, matching Steps 3 and the entry to Steps 4 — which
introduces the **preparatory move**: tactics that must be *set up* one
move ahead (lure the piece to the fork square first, open the line first,
then strike). Quiet moves inside forcing lines are now visible but
expensive; calculation stops one move too early under pressure. The
band's characteristic calculation failure is the **quiescence error**:
ending analysis on their recapture and missing the follow-up check that
changes everything.

### The thought process to teach

The Real Chess loop is assumed; what's added is **candidate discipline**
and **line hygiene**:

1. List 2–3 candidate moves *before* calculating any of them (kills the
   first-idea tunnel).
2. Order candidates by forcingness: checks, captures, threats first.
3. For the chosen line: calculate to **quiescence** — the position is
   only "done" when no checks, captures or threats remain for either
   side at the end of the line.
4. Before playing it: one last scan of the *arrival* position ("in the
   final position, what's undefended?").

## Tactical ideas to teach

- **Second-tier motifs to fluency**: deflection, decoy/attraction,
  x-ray, clearance (vacating a square/line with tempo), interference
  (blocking a defender's line), trapped piece (the win-a-piece-by-
  net family — Bg5 vs the queen, a4 vs a stranded knight on b4).
- **Zwischenzug used offensively**, not just survived — the in-between
  check inserted before recapturing as a standard tool.
- **Combining two motifs in one sequence** — the working definition of a
  combination, and the band's core puzzle diet.
- **Defensive tactics as a topic of its own**: counter-checks, desperado
  pieces (doomed piece takes something on the way out), escaping via
  counterattack ("meet a threat with a bigger threat").
- **Calculation hygiene**: list checks/captures/threats for *both* sides
  at the start of a line **and again at the end** of each line.
- The preparatory move (Steps 4's theme) in its simplest forms: luring
  ("first Qg4+ to pull the king to g8, *then* the fork works"), and
  line-opening ("first exd5 to open the e-file, then the pin wins").

## Checkmates to know

- **The Greek gift (Bxh7+), executed with its conditions checklist**:
  - White: bishop hits h7, a knight reaches g5 *with tempo* after
    ...Kxh7, the queen has the d1–h5 (or d3/c2) route, and typically a
    pawn or piece controls e5/g5 to shut the king's escape via f6;
  - Black's side of the audit: can a knight get to f6/f8 in time, is
    ...Kg6 survivable, does ...Re8 give the king luft via f8?
  - Standard follow-ups: vs Kg8 → Qh5 with Qh7#/Qxf7+ threats; vs Kg6 →
    Qg4/Qd3+ and h4–h5+ storms; vs Kh6 → Nxf7+/Qd2+ discoveries.
  Teach it as *both* an attacking weapon and a defensive checklist.
- **Boden's mate** (criss-cross bishops vs a queenside-castled king —
  the ...Qxc3+!! bxc3 Ba3# shape) and why c-pawn cover matters after
  O-O-O.
- **Légal's pattern** now executed (the Nxe5 "queen sac" against the
  pin-and-nap bishop), **hook mate** (R+N+P), **Blackburne's mate**
  (two bishops + knight vs the fianchetto-less king), **Damiano's**
  (pawn on g6, queen to h7/h5) as recognized attacking schemes.
- **Philidor's Legacy** (smothered with the Qg8+!! sac) from real
  positions, including the setup moves (Qb3/Qc4+ first).
- **Mating nets against a fleeing king**: cut the escape path *ahead* of
  the checks; the discipline of "quiet move first, checks second" in
  mating attacks.
- Opera-mate technique: back-rank mates supported by a bishop (the
  Morphy shape) — a reminder that back-rank play stays alive at every
  level, just better defended.

## Openings

Consolidation, not expansion. Guides for the band converge:

- **Depth**: main lines 5–8 moves with two or three common deviations
  covered; the classic bloat trap is learning five openings shallowly
  instead of three deeply.
- **White**: Italian matured — the c3+d4 center or the d3 slow system
  with the Nbd2–f1–g3 maneuver and the b4/a4 queenside space plans; or a
  London with *actual* understanding of when the e5 or c4 breaks come
  and what to do against early ...c5/...Qb6 counters.
- **Vs 1.e4**: 1...e5 continued (Two Knights with 5...Na5 known cold;
  the Italian's Black side), or Caro-Kann with the main plans (develop
  the c8-bishop, ...e6, ...c5 break later).
- **Vs 1.d4**: QGD or Slav structures — with the minority-attack story
  previewed (you will meet it from the Black side of the Exchange QGD).
- **The Sicilian is explicitly deferred** by multiple sources until
  ~1600: its move-order punishments and theory load buy nothing below
  that.
- New habit: after every game, find the **first moment you were out of
  book** and what the plan *should* have been (not just the move).
- Gambit policy: keep at most one sound-ish gambit for White (Evans
  Gambit is the classic recommendation under 1600 — open lines, clear
  compensation logic), and know the refutation-shaped defenses against
  the common junk gambits.

## Middlegame & strategic ideas

- **First structured planning method**: assess (material, king safety,
  piece activity, pawn structure) → pick a target → improve pieces
  toward it. Every move either serves the plan or answers a threat.
- **Imbalances vocabulary begins** (Silman's *Amateur's Mind*
  territory): bishop pair, good vs bad bishop, knight vs bishop by pawn
  structure (closed+fixed → knight; open+two-sided → bishop).
- **Open file technique**: seize it, double on it, invade on the 7th;
  and the defender's side — challenge the file, trade the invaders.
- **Attack prerequisites checklist**: stable or closed center? more
  force on that wing than defends it? lines openable at a cost you can
  pay? Pawn storms without these fail constantly here — the checklist
  converts "attacking mood" into a decision.
- **When to trade**: ahead → simplify pieces; cramped → trade to
  breathe; attacking → keep attackers, trade defenders (the "count"
  extended to trades).
- Too-materialistic correction (Pert's flag for this bracket): three
  developing moves beat a stray pawn; a rook on the 7th outweighs a
  clean extra pawn shut behind its own lines.

## Pawn structures

- **IQP from both sides now**:
  - attacker's kit: piece activity, e5 outpost for the knight, kingside
    pressure (Bc2+Qd3 batteries), the **d5 break** as the moment of
    truth;
  - defender's kit: blockade d5 (knight), trade minor pieces, aim for
    the queen ending or pure minor ending where the pawn is weak.
- **Carlsbad structure named** (from the Exchange QGD), with the
  **minority attack** as the model queenside plan: b4–b5xc6 creating a
  backward c6 pawn on a half-open file — and Black's standard counters
  (kingside piece attack, ...e5 break, the ...b5 clamp).
- **Chains**: attack the base with the right lever (...c5 and ...f6
  against the French chain d4–e5); locked centers point the attack —
  play on the side where your pawns point.
- **Passed pawn creation from a majority**: candidate pawn first, no
  premature pushes, king escorts in endings.
- Structure damage as a *choice*: doubling their pawns by taking a
  bishop pair trade-off — introduce the idea that structure and piece
  quality are currencies that trade against each other.

## Typical combinations

- **Greek gift with full follow-up** (see above) — the band's flagship
  sacrifice, both played and defended.
- **Back-rank combinations with two removals**: overload the first
  defender, deflect the second, then invade (Qe8+!-style ladders).
- **"Petite combinaison"**: two-motif, 3–5 ply sequences that net a pawn
  or the exchange rather than mate — the realistic bread and butter
  (deflect the queen from the defense of a knight; zwischenzug check
  that leaves a rook loose).
- **Philidor's Legacy** executed with the setup calculated.
- **Simplifying combinations into technically won endings** — now that
  the endings *are* technically won for them (Lucena/Philidor entering
  the toolkit).
- Trapped-piece nets as slow-motion combinations (three quiet moves
  that close the cage).

## Endgames

Silman Class D as the working set, Class C (1400–1599) previewed:

- **K+P vs K completed and tested** (the band should be perfect here);
  **key squares** as the standard language.
- **The Lucena position** ("building a bridge"): defender's king cut off
  from the pawn's file, attacking rook lifts to the 4th rank, king
  emerges, checks run out because the rook bridges at the 4th. Learn the
  *method*, then the rook-pawn caveat exists (full treatment at
  1600–1800).
- **The Philidor position** (defender's method): rook on the 6th rank
  stops the king's advance; the *moment the pawn steps to the 6th*, the
  rook drops to the 1st and checks from behind forever. Teach the
  one-sentence version; drill it against the engine.
- **Outside passed pawn** as a winning method in pawn and minor-piece
  endings (decoy the king, harvest the other wing).
- **Bishop + wrong rook-pawn draw** (corner is the wrong color → the
  defender's king reaches the corner and nothing evicts it).
- **Opposite-colored bishops**: drawing tendencies pawn-up in pure OCB
  endings (blockade on the bishop's color) — and the flip side: OCB
  *middlegames* favor the attacker.
- Q vs advanced pawn on the 7th (Silman Class C): wins against center/
  knight pawns (queen approaches with checks, king walks in), draws
  against rook/bishop pawns (stalemate resources) — the classic
  race-settling knowledge.
- Practical rook-endgame laws begin: **rook behind the passed pawn**
  (yours or theirs), rook activity over a pawn.

## Time management & practical play

- 15+10 and 30+20 as the working controls; classical (60'+) when
  available — Steps 4-level calculation doesn't fit in sub-10' games.
- Phase budgeting becomes explicit: ≤20% opening, hold 25%+ for
  moves 25–40 (where this band's games are decided).
- The **critical-moment flag**: forced sequences, all captures, and any
  move that changes the pawn structure irreversibly deserve triple the
  average think. Teach players to *notice* criticality (captures
  available, checks available, structure about to lock).
- Post-mortem timestamps: find the two moves where time and quality
  diverged worst (10-second blunder; 4-minute shuffle).

## Typical blunders (what the coach watches for)

1. Combinations attempted on excitement without the defender count
   ("unsound sacs" — Pert's miscalculated forcing lines).
2. Attacking on the flank while the center is open or opponent-owned.
3. Calculation stopping one forcing move short (quiescence errors).
4. Plan drift: five "active" moves with no common target.
5. Won rook endings drawn (rook passive, king late); pawn-up endings
   fumbled against the Philidor method.
6. Materialism: pawn-grabbing while development or king safety burns.
7. Time trouble self-inflicted by early over-thinking of quiet
   positions.

## A study week at 1200–1400

Still the 70/15/15 shape, with the middlegame slice growing:

- **Tactics**: 20–30 min/day; combination puzzles (two motifs) at
  accuracy pace; a weekly themed block on one second-tier motif;
  re-solve all misses 48h later.
- **Endgame**: two 20-minute sessions — Lucena and Philidor against the
  engine until both are 100% from both sides; one outside-passed-pawn
  position played out.
- **Openings**: patch holes from own games only; one model game per pet
  line per week, replayed twice, middlegame plan said out loud.
- **Middlegame**: one *Amateur's Mind*-style exercise weekly — take a
  quiet position from your own game, list the imbalances, state the
  plan, then compare with the engine's top idea.
- Play 3–4 slow games; annotate one thoroughly (plans, not just moves);
  play one themed sparring position if a partner exists (e.g. IQP
  middlegame from both sides).

## What a good teacher does here

Moves the ratio from "what" to "why": the student annotates their own
games with plans and gets them critiqued; candidate-move discipline is
trained (list 2–3 candidates before calculating any); an endgame
technique curriculum runs in parallel (this is the cheapest 100 points in
the band). Model games in the student's own openings replace opening-line
memorizing. Reviews now legitimately cover two items: the biggest
tactical moment and the biggest planning moment — still not five items.

## Graduation checklist (ready for 1400–1600 when…)

- [ ] Two-motif combination puzzles (~1400–1500 puzzle rating) at ~85%;
      quiescence errors rare in reviewed games.
- [ ] Greek gift: conditions recitable, one executed or correctly
      declined in own games; never suffered "for free".
- [ ] Lucena and Philidor: 100% vs engine, both sides, on demand.
- [ ] Zwischenzug played offensively at least occasionally; never lost
      to auto-recapture in the last ~20 games.
- [ ] Out-of-book moments reviewed after every game for a month; pet
      lines stable at 8–10 moves with plans attached.
- [ ] Losses tagged: pure one-move blunders near zero; the leading loss
      cause is now depth/planning, not oversight.

## Coach tuning hints (our app)

- Explanations: 4–5 ply; full tactical vocabulary bare; light positional
  terms (outpost, open file, bishop pair) used freely, structural names
  (IQP, Carlsbad) with a one-line gloss.
- Reviews may include one tactical + one positional observation; engine
  deltas in pawns are fine ("this gave back about 0.8").
- When a combination was available and missed, show the first two moves
  and name the motifs, then the punchline — don't dump the full PV.
- Endgame positions: name the theoretical pattern and whether it's won
  or drawn with best play (Lucena/Philidor literacy is expected here).
- Greek-gift geometry: whenever the Bxh7/Bxh2 sac is in the air (either
  color), surface the conditions checklist rather than a bare eval.
- Flag "criticality" moments proactively (captures/checks available,
  structure about to change) — the band under-invests thought exactly
  there.
