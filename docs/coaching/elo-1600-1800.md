## Player expectations

Rating guides describe 1600–1800 as strong club players who "understand
deep strategy and recognize tactical threats", now working to refine
positional play, endgame technique and a real repertoire. The planning
statistic flips in this band's favor: above ~1700, the player with the
clearer plan in quiet positions wins ~68% of the time — planning has
become a weapon rather than an aspiration.

Games are decided less by outright tactical accidents and more by who
mishandles the **transition moments**: opening → middlegame (the move-12
"my book ended" drift), attack → conversion (cashing an initiative into
something permanent), middlegame → endgame (the liquidation decision).
GM Pert's 1600–1900 bracket adds the band's recurring sins: wrong
exchanges, passive pieces tolerated, and the opponent's plan ignored
while executing one's own. Breaking further (to 1800+) "requires
intentional, structured study — it doesn't happen by accident".

What a typical game looks like: a genuine opening discussion 12+ moves
deep into a structure both sides know; plans launched on both wings;
the game turns on a single strategic transaction — a trade that
shouldn't have been allowed, a tension release at the wrong moment, an
exchange sacrifice one side evaluated better — and the resulting
technical phase is played to a real standard, with the defender finding
resources and the attacker needing actual technique to win.

## Rating context

1600–1800 chess.com rapid ≈ roughly 1500–1700 USCF, ≈ 1950–2100 Lichess
rapid. OTB this is a strong club regular / lower-board team player;
"Class B/A" in US terms.

## Depth of thought

Five to eight ply with growing branch discipline; Steps 5 exercises run
7–10 ply, and this band trains toward them. The limiting factor is now
**visualization accuracy** — the position five ply deep is slightly wrong
in the mind's eye (a pawn misplaced, a line unblocked), and evaluation at
the end of lines ("is this final position actually good?") becomes as
important as reaching it. De Groot's finding applies with full force from
here up: extra strength comes from *which* lines get examined and how the
end positions are judged, not from raw depth.

### The thought process to teach

The full loop is assumed; the additions are **prophylaxis and
evaluation**:

1. Every move starts with "**what does the opponent want?**" — not as a
   safety scan (that's automatic now) but as plan detection.
2. Quiet positions: imbalance audit → the position's demanded plan →
   candidate moves serving it. Forcing positions: candidates by
   forcingness, critical line verified, **stepping stones** re-scanned.
3. End-of-line **evaluation** trained explicitly: material, king
   safety, worst piece, pawn weaknesses — a 4-item scoresheet applied
   to the final position of each calculated line.
4. The transition question before any liquidation: "name the ending;
   name its result; name who's happier in it."

## Tactical ideas to teach

- **Calculation training proper**: candidate moves listed before
  analysis, forcing moves ordered first, the critical line verified to
  quiescence, stepping-stone positions held and checked. Written
  solutions to 2–3 hard positions per week (Dojo-style sweat work),
  compared against the source afterward.
- **Compound combinations** (three motifs, 6+ ply) and combinations
  with a **quiet key move** in the middle — the puzzle type that
  separates this band from 1400.
- **Defense and counterattack at parity with attack**: refuting
  speculative sacs, giving material back at the right moment to kill an
  initiative, perpetual and fortress resources hunted systematically
  when worse.
- **Intuition calibration**: when a sac "must work" by the position's
  logic (all pieces aimed, king stripped, no defenders in time) vs when
  it must be calculated to the end — and the honesty to know which case
  is on the board.
- Endgame tactics: breakthrough combinations in pawn endings, rook-
  ending tricks (skewer after promotion, checking-distance failures).

## Checkmates & attack

- **Complete attacking method against the castled king** (Vuković's
  *Art of Attack* territory): accumulation of attackers vs defenders,
  pawn levers to open lines (g4–g5, h4–h5, f4–f5), sacrifice timing —
  only when lines open AND the defender count wins.
- **Typical attacking schemes per structure**: Greek-gift families vs
  French/Caro setups; h-file batteries after opposite castling with the
  tempo-count race ("his storm vs mine — who opens a file first?");
  the f6-wedge with queen–bishop batteries; sacrifices on g7/h6 vs the
  fianchetto (Bxg7/Nf5 ideas and the dark-square binds that follow).
- **The double-bishop sacrifice (Lasker–Bauer)** learned with its exact
  conditions: Bxh7+ Kxh7, Qh5+ Kg8, Bxg7! — requires the queen+rook to
  arrive before the king escapes via f8/e7; a model of *calculated*
  double sacrifice rather than hope.
- **Defensive returns**: giving back the exchange or a pawn to kill an
  attack — the defensive mirror of every attacking scheme above.
- King hunts driven by net logic; perpetual as a weapon *and* as the
  attacker's minimum guaranteed result when sacrificing.

## Openings

Ten to twelve moves in the critical lines, but the real work is
**middlegame tabiyas**: for each repertoire branch, know the standard
plans for both sides, the typical trades to seek/avoid, and the endgames
that commonly arise.

- A second option against the main defenses appears (e.g. both a main
  line and a system vs the Sicilian), chosen to diversify structures.
- The Sicilian, King's Indian and other theory-heavy complexes are now
  viable projects — entered via model games and structure study, not
  memorization first.
- **Novelty handling** is taught: out of book, reconstruct the ideas
  (what's the structure? where are the breaks? which piece is the
  problem?) rather than panic.
- Personal repertoire files begin: one page per line — critical moves,
  the tabiya diagram, three plans, one model game reference.
- Preparation habit: after every serious game, the opening phase gets
  five minutes of file-patching the same week.

## Middlegame & strategic ideas

- **Prophylactic thinking as standard operating procedure** (every
  move: what does the opponent want? — Karpov model games as the
  textbook; the lichess/Chessable prophylaxis literature is aimed
  exactly here).
- **The principle of two weaknesses**: fix one weakness, create a
  second, stretch the defense between them — the standard winning
  method in better positions from here to master level.
- **Maneuvering**: multi-move piece routes (the Nf3–d2–f1–g3 family,
  the "bad bishop around the chain" journeys) chosen by target squares,
  not adjacency.
- **Static vs dynamic assessment**: trading a structural concession for
  initiative and knowing the initiative's shelf life ("three moves of
  attack or it's just worse structure").
- **Advantage transformation**: converting a better structure into a
  won ending, or space into a kingside attack, at the right moment —
  the skill Pert's bracket calls "when to exchange".
- Restriction play: improving your position while denying the
  opponent's *plan* (not just their moves) — the bridge to 1800+
  prophylaxis.

## Pawn structures

Full family fluency with typical piece placements per structure:

- **Carlsbad** (both plans + the Nf1/Ne5 piece schemes and Black's
  ...Ne4 resource), **IQP**, **hanging pawns**, **Maroczy bind** (space
  vs the freeing ...b5/...f5 breaks — patience as the bind-holder's
  skill), **Hedgehog** (the ...b5/...d5 springs and why the hedgehog
  bites impatient attackers), **French and KID chains** with the
  standard regrouping schemes, **Stonewall** ideas (the e5-square
  economy, the bad bishop's routes).
- **Structure-driven trade policy**: which minor piece each structure
  wants to keep ("in the Carlsbad, White's light-squared bishop is the
  attack; in the French, Black's light-squared bishop is the problem").
- Doubled/backward pawns re-evaluated: when they're strengths (open
  files, central control, square coverage).
- Structure *transformations* previewed: IQP → hanging pawns → isolated
  pair; Carlsbad → symmetric open c-file battles — each transformation
  re-deals the plans.

## Typical combinations

- The **positional exchange sacrifice** as a real weapon (dark-square
  domination, destroying the pawn shield, killing a blockader — the
  Sicilian ...Rxc3 as the canonical example), evaluated by compensation
  categories rather than counted material.
- **Pawn breaks as combinations**: d5/e5/f5 thrusts calculated as
  forcing sequences with piece activity as the payoff.
- **Attack-to-endgame transformations**: sacrifice, regain with
  interest, convert the superior ending — the mature form of "winning
  an attack".
- **Long liquidations verified to the true result** of the resulting
  theoretical ending (this requires the Class A/B endgame knowledge
  below — the combination is only as sound as the ending it enters).
- Quiet-key-move combinations: the Rf3–h3 lift *inside* a forcing
  sequence; the prophylactic Kh1 before the storm.

## Endgames

Silman Class B completed, Class A (1800–1999) entered from below; *100
Endgames You Must Know* becomes the working reference:

- **Triangulation, outflanking** and zugzwang engineering in pawn
  endings; "strange races" (Class A) previewed.
- **Lucena with a rook-pawn** (the extra conditions), **rook in front
  of its own 7th-rank pawn** (the classic grief position — checking
  distance, the king-walk plan), **R+2 connected pawns vs R** converted
  cleanly.
- **Vancura defense** introduced (rook vs rook + a-pawn: side checks
  against the pawn from the flank) — recognition level now, automatic
  by 1800–2000.
- **Complex opposite-colored-bishop endings**: bishop + two connected
  passers (the two-diagonal rule), fortress construction and its
  failure modes.
- **Practical technique drilled to habit**: rook activity over
  material, king activation as the first endgame move, cutting off by
  files vs ranks, outside-passer decoys, "do not hurry".
- Sparring diet (Dojo-style): set positions played out against a
  partner/engine — R+2 vs R, OCB grinds, minority-attack endings.

## Time management & practical play

- 30+ minute games standard; classical OTB where available. Blitz is
  explicitly recreational from here (guides above 1500 agree: plateaus
  break with more slow chess and analysis, not more blitz).
- Phase budgets are now personal data: track where *your* games are
  decided and bank time for those moves.
- Clock as evaluation input: choosing the practical (simpler, slightly
  worse) line in mutual time trouble is correct play, not cowardice —
  and needs to be a *decision*.
- Post-mistake ritual formalized; composure after the first error is
  worth ~50 points in this band by common coaching estimate.

## Typical blunders (what the coach watches for)

1. Strategic overreach: pressing a small edge into a lost position.
2. Wrong trades: giving up the structure-critical piece (the
   Pert-bracket signature).
3. Missing the *onset* of opponent counterplay while executing a plan.
4. Conversion failures: winning positions allowed to re-complicate.
5. Deep-line evaluation errors (reaches the position, misjudges it).
6. Opening-model dependence: fine in known structures, adrift in
   unfamiliar ones.
7. Passive pieces tolerated for the sake of a plan ("the knight was
   coming back… in six moves").

## A study week at 1600–1800

The 50/30/10/10 split bent toward the diagnosed leak:

- **Calculation (~50%)**: hard puzzle sets (compound combinations,
  defense, quiet key moves) at full-effort pace 3×/week; written-line
  solving on 2–3 studies or master-game positions; every own-game
  complication re-calculated on the board.
- **Openings (~30%)**: repertoire-file building (one branch per week to
  tabiya standard: plans, trades, model game); annotated master games
  in your structures — 2 per week, questions asked ("why this trade?
  where's the break?").
- **Middlegame (~10%)**: HTRYC chapters; prophylaxis training (cover
  the moves of a Karpov game, predict the *opponent's* plan each move).
- **Endgame (~10%)**: Class B/A items as sparring positions against the
  engine; one 100-Endgames item per week to automatic.
- Play 2–4 slow games; annotate one to Dojo standard (own thoughts
  first, engine second); maintain the loss-cause statistics that decide
  next week's bend.

## What a good teacher does here

Diagnoses statistically: many games reviewed for *recurring* weakness
classes (complications? conversions? specific structures?) and training
targets the top one. Endgame technique is pushed to automatism with
sparring positions (R+2 vs R, OCB grinds). Annotated master-game study
in the student's structures replaces general strategy books. Psychology
enters: time budgeting per phase, composure after the first mistake.
Feedback can be direct; the student can absorb "your plan was wrong,
here's the position's plan" without sugar.

## Graduation checklist (ready for 1800–2000 when…)

- [ ] Prophylactic question asked on most moves of a reviewed game
      (audit: the player can narrate the opponent's plan at any point).
- [ ] Two-weaknesses method executed in at least one won game (fix,
      stretch, invade — narratable afterward).
- [ ] Exchange-sacrifice positions evaluated correctly more often than
      not (test set); compensation vocabulary in active use.
- [ ] Lucena rook-pawn cases, rook-in-front-of-pawn, and Vancura
      recognized instantly; R+2 vs R never fumbled.
- [ ] Repertoire files exist for every main branch with tabiya + plans
      + model game; out-of-book drift no longer a loss cause in the
      stats.
- [ ] Conversion: won positions (+2 or better at move 30) actually won
      at ≥80% over a meaningful sample.

## Coach tuning hints (our app)

- Explanations: 6–8 ply; unrestricted positional vocabulary; structures
  referenced by name without gloss.
- Highest-value coach behaviors: state the opponent's plan every time
  (prophylaxis training), name the demanded plan for the structure,
  flag transition moments ("this trade turns it into a Class-A rook
  ending — check Lucena applicability before simplifying").
- Show full refutation branches for miscalculations; give evals as
  deltas and absolutes freely.
- Praise should target judgment calls (right trade, right moment to
  transform) — move-level praise is condescending here.
- Counterplay radar: when the player is executing a plan, surface the
  opponent's fastest counter-idea and its move count ("your b5 needs
  three moves; his f4–f3 arrives in two").
