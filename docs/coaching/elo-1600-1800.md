# 1600–1800 · Strong club player

[← 1400–1600](elo-1400-1600.md) · [index](README.md) · [next band →](elo-1800-2000.md)

## Player expectations

Rating guides describe 1600–1800 as strong club players who "understand
deep strategy and recognize tactical threats", now working to refine
positional play, endgame technique and a real repertoire. The planning
statistic flips in this band's favor: above ~1700, the player with the
clearer plan in quiet positions wins ~68% of the time — planning has
become a weapon. Games are decided less by outright tactical accidents
and more by who mishandles the transition moments: opening → middlegame,
attack → conversion, middlegame → endgame. Breaking further (to 1800+)
"requires intentional, structured study — it doesn't happen by accident".

## Depth of thought

Five to eight ply with growing branch discipline; Steps 5 exercises (aimed
at ~1900) run 7–10 ply, and this band trains toward them. The limiting
factor is now **visualization accuracy** — the position five ply deep is
slightly wrong in the mind's eye (a pawn misplaced, a line unblocked), and
evaluation at the end of lines ("is this final position actually good?")
becomes as important as reaching it.

## Tactical ideas to teach

- Calculation *training* proper: candidate moves listed before analysis,
  forcing moves ordered first, the critical line verified to quiescence,
  stepping-stone positions held and checked.
- Compound combinations (three motifs, 6+ ply) and combinations with a
  **quiet key move** in the middle.
- Defense and counterattack at parity with attack: refuting speculative
  sacs, giving material back at the right moment, perpetual and fortress
  resources.
- Intuition calibration: when a sac "must work" by the position's logic
  vs when it must be calculated to the end.

## Checkmates & attack

- Complete attacking method against the castled king: accumulation of
  attackers vs defenders, pawn levers to open lines (g4–g5, h4–h5, f4–f5),
  sacrifice timing (only when lines open AND the defender count wins).
- Typical attacking schemes **per structure**: Greek-gift families vs the
  French/Caro setups, h-file batteries after opposite castling, the
  f6-wedge with queen–bishop batteries, sacrifices on g7/h6 vs fianchetto.
- Defensive returns: giving back the exchange or a pawn to kill an attack.

## Openings

Ten to twelve moves in the critical lines, but the real work is
**middlegame tabiyas**: for each repertoire branch, know the standard
plans for both sides, the typical trades to seek/avoid, and the endgames
that commonly arise. A second option against the main defenses appears
(e.g. both a main line and a system vs the Sicilian). The Sicilian, King's
Indian and other theory-heavy complexes are now viable projects. Novelty
handling is taught: out of book, reconstruct the *ideas* rather than
panic.

## Middlegame & strategic ideas

- **Prophylactic thinking as standard operating procedure** (every move:
  what does the opponent want; Karpov model games).
- **The principle of two weaknesses**: fix one weakness, create a second,
  stretch the defense between them.
- Maneuvering: multi-move piece routes (the Nf3–d2–f1–g3 family) chosen by
  target squares, not adjacency.
- Static vs dynamic assessment: trading a structural concession for
  initiative and knowing the shelf life of the initiative.
- Advantage transformation: converting a better structure into a won
  ending, or space into a kingside attack, at the right moment.

## Pawn structures

Full family fluency with typical piece placements per structure:

- Carlsbad (both plans + the Nf1/Ne5 piece schemes), IQP, hanging pawns,
  **Maroczy bind** (space vs the freeing breaks b5/f5), **Hedgehog**
  (patience and the ...b5/...d5 springs), French and KID chains with the
  standard regrouping schemes, Stonewall ideas.
- Structure-driven trade policy: which minor piece each structure wants
  to keep; "bad bishop inside vs outside the chain".
- Doubled/backward pawns re-evaluated: when they're strengths (open
  files, central control).

## Typical combinations

- The **positional exchange sacrifice** as a real weapon (dark-square
  domination, destroying the pawn shield, killing a blockade).
- Pawn breaks as combinations: d5/e5/f5 thrusts calculated as forcing
  sequences.
- Attack-to-endgame transformations: sacrifice, regain with interest,
  convert the superior ending.
- Long liquidations verified to the resulting theoretical ending's true
  result (this requires the Silman Class A/B knowledge below).

## Endgames

Silman Class A (1800–1999) entered from below: "strange races", K+P vs
K+P precision, **Lucena with a rook-pawn**, rook in front of its own
7th-rank pawn, complex opposite-colored-bishop endings (bishop + two
connected passers). Practical technique drilled to habit: rook activity
over material, king activation as the first endgame move, cutting the
king off, outside-passer decoys, "do not hurry".

## Typical blunders (what the coach watches for)

1. Strategic overreach: pressing a small edge into a lost position.
2. Wrong trades: giving up the structure-critical piece.
3. Missing the *onset* of opponent counterplay while executing a plan.
4. Conversion failures: winning positions allowed to re-complicate.
5. Deep-line evaluation errors (reaches the position, misjudges it).
6. Opening-model dependence: fine in known structures, adrift in
   unfamiliar ones.

## What a good teacher does here

Diagnoses statistically: many games reviewed for *recurring* weakness
classes (complications? conversions? specific structures?) and training
targets the top one. Endgame technique is pushed to automatism with
sparring positions (R+2 vs R, OCB grinds). Annotated master-game study in
the student's structures replaces general strategy books. Psychology
enters: time budgeting per phase, composure after the first mistake.
Feedback can be direct; the student can absorb "your plan was wrong,
here's the position's plan" without sugar.

## Coach tuning hints (our app)

- Explanations: 6–8 ply; unrestricted positional vocabulary; structures
  referenced by name without gloss.
- Highest-value coach behaviors: state the opponent's plan every time
  (prophylaxis training), name the demanded plan for the structure, flag
  transition moments ("this trade turns it into a Class-A rook ending —
  check Lucena applicability before simplifying").
- Show full refutation branches for miscalculations; give evals as deltas
  and absolutes freely.
- Praise should target judgment calls (right trade, right moment to
  transform) — move-level praise is condescending here.
